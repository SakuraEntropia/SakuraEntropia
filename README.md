## 📜 Preface: Some Thoughts on Humans and Existing Social Systems

*A living essay. First published August 2026. Last updated November 19, 2026.*

## The promised future

We were once promised a future like this: material abundance, humanity reaching UBI, commuting in eVTOLs, kilometer-scale megastructures, ring-shaped space stations, robots taking care of everything for everyone, all labor handed over to machines, and resources distributed on demand.

Of course, if anyone posts that vision online today, they will only be mocked: "keep dreaming — anything exists in dreams." That is what happens when an ideal runs into productivity and chaotic systems. The modern system is still steered by human emotion. But that mockery is doing more work than it admits. It is not only a judgment about the world. It is also a defense of a particular way of living in the world.

Here is the question I keep coming back to. We have built machines that fly, compute, translate, draw and diagnose. A civilization with our productive power should be able to ask a very different question than "how do I survive". And yet most people I know, including myself on most days, organize their entire lives around that question anyway. Why does a civilization that could move toward abundance still demand that almost everyone sell their labor time in order to live? Why, when technology keeps lowering the amount of labor that survival requires, does the culture still insist on the same path: study, find a job, buy or rent a home, marry, have children, retire? And underneath both: is realism helping people adapt to reality, or is it sometimes dressing up the present as the only possible reality? This essay is my attempt to walk through that chain honestly, without pretending I have solved it.

## What a great civilization means

Start with what a great civilization would even mean. My definition of a Type I civilization is concrete: it can reach any place on this planet, reshape any geography on this planet, travel to and from Mars at will, and cure almost all diseases. On that scale, progress is not a feeling but a checklist, and we are already checking items. The Eurasian Pole of Inaccessibility was once a place that defeated the ancients, but we have built Tianshan International Airport nearby — a 4F-class airport. Shenzhen was once overrun with cockroaches; no one in ancient times could have predicted that it would become a technology capital. To be an outstanding civilization, we need at least an elevator up Everest, and the ability to take everyone to Lop Nur, the Amazon — anywhere. Anyone should be able to reach Mars smoothly. Musk and entrepreneurs like him are pushing in this direction, but they also face many difficulties imposed by the existing system.

There is a gap between those two kinds of sentences. One kind is about what humanity as a whole can do. The other kind is about what an individual human is allowed to want. We can pour enough concrete to build a 4F airport at the pole of inaccessibility, and still tell an individual who wants to attempt something large to first secure a job, an apartment, a loan, a stable position. The civilization advances; the people inside it are asked to stay in place. I think that gap is the subject of everything that follows.

## The ordinary person

Begin with the person inside the structure. As for the definition of an "ordinary person" — that is a very fuzzy thing. Any person in any field can be called ordinary: Musk is undoubtedly one of the world's best in business, but he may know nothing about sports. Let me give a concrete definition: a non-outstanding employee at an ordinary company, earning around the average income, who must keep selling their labor to survive, follows the fixed life path of marriage, children, and retirement, and has no thought of self-realization such as starting a business.

But the word "ordinary person" has now become something close to a "Dalit" online — a status an individual basically cannot escape in their lifetime. I admit that in the current economic downturn, most people will suffer lost opportunities and debt servitude, and will live out their lives step by step along the fixed path.

And here I have to be careful, because two different claims are hiding inside that word, and they must be separated. Ordinary is not worthless. A person who never becomes an entrepreneur, a scientist, an artist, an athlete or a public figure has not failed at being human. The problem is not that most people are ordinary. The problem is that "ordinary" has been redefined as a caste. I am not asking for a world where everyone must become extraordinary; I do not believe that, and I do not want it. A truly free society would not require anyone to become a genius in order to deserve the right to choose their own way of living. The target is not "everyone must become extraordinary". The target is "no one has to become extraordinary just to deserve autonomy".

## Distribution

Why is the path fixed? The distribution of resources in our society is extremely imbalanced. As of 2026, humanity is still under capitalism. This is not about good people versus bad people — it is about distribution, and the distribution is skewed by design. Most people own no means of production, so they must sell their labor power to survive, and the wage they receive covers only the reproduction of that labor: housing, transport, food. That is exactly why those costs swallow most of a person's income. Nobody needs to plot against you — the system only needs you to keep working, not to excel. The surplus your labor creates is accumulated as capital and concentrated in fewer and fewer hands, and that concentrated capital decides what gets funded and who gets the material conditions to pursue excellence. A single breakthrough costs millions — nearly a lifetime's income for an ordinary person — so for most people the door is shut before they ever reach it. Entrepreneurs aren't the villains; they are the ones holding the accumulated surplus, operating inside the same structure. But the structure itself is not innocent: it turns society's surplus into a gate that only a few get to pass. I'm not here to blame individuals. I'm saying that as long as the social surplus flows through capital accumulation, what most people can become is decided by how that surplus is distributed — and right now, that distribution mocks effort.

Why, then, don't more people try to start something, or to become excellent? First, their economic conditions may simply not allow it. Second, the risk is too high. Third, they are shaped by the social environment around them. Fourth, their own ability may not yet have crossed the threshold. These aren't excuses — they are the actual mechanics.

## The pyramid of needs

Those are the constraints. But constraints alone do not explain the culture, so let me look at the layer of needs underneath them.

Maslow's pyramid is too neat to be literally true, but as a sketch it is useful. A person whose survival is not secured cannot easily be free: free to start a company, to do basic science, to make art, to research one question for years, to explore the unknown, to pursue self-actualization. Without a safety net, those things are luxuries in the strictest sense. So survival-first is not naturally wrong. For an ordinary person in a highly scarce environment, realism may be the most rational survival strategy available, and I would never tell such a person they are thinking too small.

But now ask the same question at the level of a civilization. If housing, food, energy, healthcare, education and automated production keep getting more abundant, then survival should gradually stop occupying most of a human life, and the goal of a society should shift from "how do we keep people alive" to "what can people do once they are alive". That is the movement the pyramid describes: survival, security, belonging, esteem, self-actualization. Seen this way, the meaning of technological progress is not just higher efficiency. It is helping more and more people move up from the bottom of the pyramid. A civilization's true wealth is not the number of goods it produces; it is the number of people who do not have to sell their entire lives in order to survive.

## Two stories about a human life

There are two stories a society can tell about a human life. The first is the survival narrative: find a job, earn money, buy or rent a home, marry, have children, support your parents, retire. It answers a single question — how does one person stay safe inside the existing structure? It is not wrong. For most people, through most of history, it was the only narrative the material base could afford.

The second is the civilization narrative: why do we develop technology at all? Why automation, AI, robots, energy, spaceflight? What, exactly, should civilization be liberating human beings from? Hannah Arendt separated labor, the work that keeps life going, from action, the capacity to begin something new. A civilization that organizes everyone around labor has forgotten what it wanted to be free from. The civilization narrative is the reason the survival narrative should be allowed to shrink. If centuries of industrial revolution, computing, AI and robotics end with the message "congratulations — you can now find a job more efficiently", then we have missed the deepest meaning of the productivity revolution. A rich civilization eventually has to answer not only how people survive, but what survival is for. When machines can do more and more of the work that keeps society running, should selling your labor time remain the precondition for the right to exist? I do not have an answer. I only refuse to accept that the current arrangement is a law of nature. It is a choice, and the distance between the two narratives is where most of modern exhaustion lives.

## The ROI of a life

Between the two narratives sits a very common philosophy of life. Choose a major by employment rate. Judge a hobby by its salary. Judge study by its ROI. Do research only if it can be monetized. Start a company only if it can raise funding. Judge art by its commercial value. A human life, compressed into an ROI optimization problem.

I do not want to condemn this thinking, because in a resource-scarce environment it is genuinely rational. If a family has no safety net, no capital, no tolerance for failure, no guaranteed healthcare, housing or education, then "think about employment and income first" is not cowardice. It is arithmetic. The problem appears when productivity has already reduced material scarcity substantially, and the survival logic remains the highest principle of life anyway.

I find it useful to separate three layers. The first is survival rationality: "I must earn money or I cannot live." That is a real constraint, not a philosophy. The second is instrumental rationality: "everything I do must prove its economic value." That is what happens when the logic of the market enters a person's psychology; a constraint becomes an identity. Weber drew this distinction a century ago: instrumental rationality, the logic of means, against value rationality, the choice of ends. The third layer is that value rationality: some things are worth doing even if they will never maximize income. Basic science. Art. Philosophy. Exploration. Games. Personal creation. Pure curiosity. Public knowledge with no direct economic return.

And notice what happens when instrumental rationality becomes the only language available. "Why do you study mathematics?" — "Because the salary is high", and not "because I want to know mathematics". "Why do you make art?" — "Because it makes money", and not "because I want to create". The market logic has crossed from the economy into the personality. The question, then, is when a civilization becomes rich enough to let people ask "what is worth doing" rather than only "what pays the most". That is not an economic question. It is the question of what an economy is for.

## The Zhang Xuefeng-style narrative

In China this survival-first thinking has a name and a face, and it is enormously popular. Zhang Xuefeng is only the most visible of a whole genre of education and career bloggers, and I am not interested in judging him; I am interested in the narrative. Call it the Zhang Xuefeng-style narrative: don't talk about empty ideals; first think about employment, income, which city, which major, the stability of a state post (编制), and family risk. Its rule is simple: interest can wait, survive first.

The narrative is popular for reasons that deserve respect. High education costs. High housing prices. Education competition that begins in childhood. Employment pressure. Families without safety nets. Anxiety about class mobility. A cost of failure so high that one mistake can set a family back for years. Young people with no trial-and-error capital. Parents who avoid risk on behalf of their children. A system in which your degree is hard-bound to your employment. In a low-welfare, low-safety-net society with expensive housing and education, "find a job that can feed you first" is extremely reasonable advice. I would probably give that advice myself in many cases.

But there is a problem hiding in plain sight: a survival strategy slowly becomes a philosophy of life. "You should find a job first" slides, without anyone noticing, into "your life was always supposed to be a job". Those are two completely different propositions. The first is advice about sequencing. The second is a verdict about what a human being is for.

## Homejoy and the limits of realism

Let me test this against a concrete failure, because failures are where realism sounds most convincing. Homejoy was a YC company that did on-demand home cleaning. It grew fast, raised real money, got a great deal of attention, and shut down in 2015. It is usually remembered as a lesson in realism. The market was real and brutal: customer churn, thin margins, cleaners who had to be found, kept and classified, competition on every side, growth that cost more than it returned, unit economics that never closed, cash that burned faster than the story could raise more. A founder who ignores that list is not an idealist. They have simply lost the ability to receive feedback.

So Homejoy answers a real question: when should a person accept reality? The answer is: constantly, on the operational level. Realism is not the enemy of entrepreneurship; it is the material of it. Ideals need reality for feedback. Without reality, an ideal becomes a hallucination. But the reverse is also true: reality should not become the final boundary of the ideal. Without ideals, reality becomes a cage. Homejoy died because its economics were wrong, and that was reality delivering the correct verdict on that company. The wrong move is to extend the verdict: to conclude from Homejoy that no one should attempt anything hard, that every ambitious plan is a Homejoy waiting to be exposed. The company failed. The question of what a company can be did not.

## The scales of reality

Here is where a strange contrast becomes useful. Sam Altman taught much of the early YC canon: the startup lecture series at Stanford in 2014, the essays, the endless advice. The picture of reality in those talks was deliberately small: find a problem, build a product, get users, grow, don't die, build a company. That is reality at the scale of a single founder. The same person now runs an organization whose decisions touch AGI, AI infrastructure, scientific research, compute, energy, geopolitics, and what technology does to a civilization as a whole. I am not calling him a hypocrite. I am pointing at something more interesting: reality itself has scales. For a college student, "find a job" is reality. For a founder, "will the company survive" is reality. For a civilization, "can energy, computation, automation, medicine and spaceflight change what is possible" is reality. Each scale has its own realism, and each is real. The error is to take the realism of one scale and absolutize it into the realism of every scale. A society that only allows people to discuss how to live better inside the existing rules, and never whether the rules themselves can change, has lost its imagination at the scale of a civilization.

This is also where the refrain "don't get above yourself" gets interesting. A young person says "I will build a great company", and the environment hears arrogance. The same person, twenty years later, actually runs a large organization, and gets to speak about how AI, energy and automation will change society — and everyone calls it vision. So which is being punished: the arrogance, or the lack of proof? Watch how the words change around the same ambition. Before success: arrogant, unrealistic, delusional. After success: visionary, ambitious, world-changing. Society, it turns out, is often not against ambition at all. It is against unverified ambition. And "don't get above yourself" does have a statistical basis: most people who believe they are the exception are not the exception. But that fact cannot be stretched into the command "don't try to become the exception". The first is a statistic. The second is a social order, and the step between them is where the culture does its work.

## Cynicism

Underneath all of this runs cynicism. I do not mean pessimism. Modern cynicism is something more specific: a person knows reality is cruel, and treats accepting that reality as maturity. "Your startup will most likely fail." "Your odds of becoming a top scientist are tiny." "You are, in the overwhelming majority of cases, an ordinary person." "The world is unfair." "Ideals cannot pay rent." Many of these statements are true. That is exactly what makes cynicism dangerous: it often describes reality correctly and then derives action wrongly.

Look at the logic closely. Most people fail. Therefore I should not try. Does the second sentence follow from the first? No. "Most people fail" is a statistical fact; it does not imply "therefore you should not try". Those are different claims, and the second smuggles itself in as if the first had proven it. Statistics describe the majority; they do not dictate the destiny of an individual. And the fact that most attempts fail does not imply that attempting is irrational.

There is a deeper conflict here, between statistical rationality and individual rationality. If a person always decides their actions by aggregate probabilities, they will almost certainly never become an exception of any kind, because every exception, before it became an exception, had to take some action that was not the optimal strategy for the population average. The person who becomes world-class did something the statistics told them not to do. Applied to the individual, the logic of the aggregate is circular: it produces exactly the world it predicts. A civilization in which everyone adopts the safest available strategy produces no paradigm shifts; it only refines the ones it already has. Statistics can tell you the risk. It cannot decide the value for you.

But I want to say this without any chicken soup. Most startups do fail. Most research attempts never become breakthroughs. Luck matters enormously. Ability differences are real. Resource differences are real. Self-belief does not change the objective probabilities. Anti-cynicism is not the belief that you will win. It is knowing you might fail, and still refusing to delete every non-necessary possibility in advance because the failure probability is high. Camus stared at the same absurdity and refused to hand it his resignation; one must imagine Sisyphus happy, he wrote, and that sentence is the exact opposite of cynicism.

## Don't get above yourself

This is where the logic gets personal. A young person says: "I want to start a company." "I want to do world-class research." "I want to make art." "I want to change an industry." "I want to create something that never existed." And the environment answers instantly, with statistics: "Who do you think you are?" "Do you know how many people have tried this?" "Do you know the success rate?" "Get a job first." "Stop dreaming." The refrain has a name: 不要自命不凡, don't get above yourself.

There is a subtle logical error in this: using the low success rate to negate the attempt itself. "Most people fail" is not the same proposition as "this particular person will fail". "Many people have already done this" is not the same as "you cannot be the first to complete a new combination". And "becoming a world-class figure is hard" does not contradict "creating a world first in a locally defined sense can be surprisingly easy".

## World first

The numbers are stranger than the refrain admits. Climbing Everest requires very strict permits, enormous costs, and many failed attempts — yet the number of people who have stood on the summit still exceeds 11,000. Athletes, directors, game producers, esports players, stars, singers, entrepreneurs, mathematicians, politicians, physicists, other scientists, musicians, painters, artists... counted together, they form a huge number. If you define "world first" in a narrow enough way, being world first is not that hard: the world's first person to do tensor computation with an audio equalizer; the world's first person to train JEPA in Minecraft — then you just go and do it. The examples sound like jokes, but the proposition underneath them is serious. The space of human knowledge is indefinitely large. Define a specific enough problem, and new frontiers keep appearing. Being world first in a tiny domain is not as hard as it sounds; the hard part is finding the question. Creation is not climbing an existing leaderboard. It is building new axes. And once I started thinking of axes that way, I wanted to know how many people in all of human history ever built one that lasted.

## Counting the people who changed the world

At some point I tried to count this directly, using databases of historical figures, Wikipedia and estimates of how many humans have ever lived. I counted scientists, mathematicians, inventors and engineers; founders and industrialists; writers, cartoonists, directors, animators, artists and musicians; athletes, esports players and competition champions; politicians, explorers and everyone else with historical influence. Together they contain a surprisingly large number of remarkable people — against all the humans who have ever lived, almost nothing. At the scale of tens or hundreds of billions of lives, how scarce is real achievement?

My main instrument was the Pantheon project. [Pantheon 1.0](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata201575) (Yu et al., 2016) is a manually verified dataset of globally famous biographies: 11,341 people with biographies in more than 25 Wikipedia language editions, measured through language coverage and pageviews. [Pantheon 2.0](https://pantheon.world/data/datasets), in its 2025 public version, holds around 126,582 records; its Historical Popularity Index measures cross-language fame in public memory, not actual contribution to civilization. A separate study, [A cross-verified database of notable people, 3500BC–2018AD](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-022-01369-4) (Laouenan et al., 2022), assembled roughly 2.29 million cross-source notable people and compared them with the cumulative historical population. It is independent of Pantheon, and its numbers cannot be treated as statistics under the same standard.

These databases are full of statistical bias, and the most important sentence of this whole exercise is: being remembered is not the same as changing the world. Fame bias: someone remembered because of politics, entertainment, sport, exposure or internet transmission has not made a contribution of equivalent size; an unknown engineer who changed a key technology can be far less famous than a pop star. Underrepresentation: many ancient people left no reliable record, and some breakthroughs were made by people history never named; the record is uneven across language, region, class, gender and era, so the databases miss many genuinely important people. Documentation bias: media, publishing, the internet and business databases have made more and more people searchable, which does not give everyone recorded the same historical influence. Measurement and definition bias: language counts, pageviews and attention are only proxies; even if the HPI measures cross-cultural fame, it cannot prove how much causal contribution someone made, and inclusion thresholds differ enormously between databases. Survivorship bias: we see the people who succeeded, got recorded and received recognition; those with the same talent and effort stopped by war, economic conditions, institutional barriers, the limits of their era or plain accident mostly do not appear at all. These databases record ability, but they also record opportunity and chance.

So I made my own rough correction. Taking every famous person as someone who truly changed history is a serious overcount; raising the definition from "famous" to "made an outstanding, actual, lasting contribution to science, technology, economics, art, culture or social development" removes a large share of the records. But missing records mean many real contributors never entered the databases either. The correction has to run in both directions: exclude the merely famous, add back the forgotten contributors, separate original contribution from professional success. My own tentative estimate, after considering both the overinclusion of merely famous individuals and the underrepresentation of genuinely consequential contributors, is somewhere around 200,000 to 250,000 people across human history. It is not Pantheon's conclusion and not a statistically validated result; it is my exploratory estimate, and I have no evidence to give it a reliable confidence interval. I am not pretending that a correction coefficient or a formula produced it. There is no objective boundary line for historical importance, and I am not saying that only 200,000 to 250,000 people in history deserved respect, nor that Pantheon contains 200,000 to 250,000 people. I am counting a strict sense of historical contribution: not human dignity, not the value of a life, not professional status.

What these numbers did to me is harder to describe. When I actually looked at the data, the abstract sentence "the vast majority of people will never leave a name in history" suddenly acquired a concrete quantity, and it made me depressed, close to despair. I cannot easily accept a life arranged in advance: enter university, graduate, become an ordinary employee at some company, sell labor time for decades, retire, and then disappear from collective memory like the overwhelming majority of humans before me. I understand this kind of life is not failure. But I hold my own life to a different standard: I do not want to accept, before seriously trying to create something genuinely unique, that I am only ever going to be a statistical ordinary person. I have to separate three levels. Autonomy: economic independence, not depending my whole life on selling labor time to an organization. Creation: making something with real scientific, technical, artistic or social value. Historical legacy: making something that keeps influencing later generations and keeps my name in history. The three are connected but not the same, and I admit the third has a strong pull on me. Historical memory depends on luck, transmission and the judgment of later ages, and is not fully under anyone's control. That conflict between rational knowledge and personal desire is one source of my existential anxiety.

There is a historical paradox here. As population, education, research systems, the internet and global industry expand, the number of people with outstanding ability and notable achievement may keep growing. At the same time, frontier research in many mature fields requires more and more knowledge, funding, equipment and organized collaboration; a young person who could have made an important breakthrough alone decades ago may now face a far larger body of knowledge and far more mature competitors. But the opportunity structure differs by field and by stage of technological revolution, and new technologies can also lower old thresholds and open new directions. The success probabilities of the past, today's competition thresholds and tomorrow's new opportunities are three different questions, and they have to be analyzed separately.

Which brings me back. I believe an ideal civilization should not require everyone to become extraordinary in order to deserve dignity, freedom and a secure life; an ordinary employee should also have the right to freely choose their own life. And yet I personally refuse to accept a lifetime of ordinariness. A society should allow ordinary people to remain ordinary freely, and it should also allow people who want to be extraordinary to pursue it seriously. These two positions do not contradict each other: the first is my demand on the institutions, the second is my demand on my own life. I am against a society that treats winners as the only people who deserve respect, and against a society that uses statistical probability to argue that a young person should not try to change their fate. Even if I end up failing, the attempt itself will not have been worthless — though I am not saying failure is noble, or that effort deserves success: effort must accept reality's feedback, and an attempt has to account for its risks and costs. Statistical rationality describes populations; individual rationality decides actions. Survival rationality answers how I stay secure; value rationality answers what is worth doing. Freedom is a right; achievement is a wager. Respect reality, but don't worship it.

I met Yann LeCun once, at a public lecture at Nanyang Technological University in Singapore. He is one of the most influential scientists in the history of deep learning and a Turing Award winner. Before that afternoon he was, for me, a name in papers and tech news, someone at a distance ordinary people do not cross. Then I sat in the same lecture hall and got the chance to ask him a question and exchange a few words. He did not feel like a myth. What I felt instead was a thought that sounds disrespectful and is not meant to be: he was just a very, very lucky ordinary person. Ordinary does not mean his research was ordinary; it means that when a great figure steps out of the books and stands in front of you, you notice he is a concrete person. He did not know at birth that he would win a Turing Award; he was once a young student in a world that did not yet know where technology would go. His success rests on ability, long-term investment, choices, collaboration, and the technological conditions of a particular era. And inside it there is luck that cannot be ignored: the direction he chose happened to become a foundation of modern AI, and history never guaranteed the same opportunity to everyone with equal passion and potential. I am not claiming to understand his decades of research from one brief meeting, or reducing his contribution to luck; this is only the impression of a person who stood close to a name for a few minutes.

The other side of the experience is this: I have met people who made me feel the exact opposite. One of them was a student in a neighboring department. As I understand it, he had once been in his subject's national olympiad team, had studied university physics and music theory, reached the tenth level in vocal music, and had absolute pitch. When he touched the material of our mathematics department, he seemed to understand things that cost me hours of effort as effortlessly as drinking water. He also worked with two research groups, one in materials science and one in machine learning, and I was told a professor had described him as one of the best researchers he had ever seen. I am not inventing his name or records, and not turning him into a superhuman; these are a classmate's impressions, not verified facts. What they forced me to accept is simpler and harder: some people's learning ability really does cross many very different fields, and I cannot hold the easy egalitarian story that innate cognitive ability is basically equal and that the same effort produces the same results.

Talent exists, and its distribution is cruel. Memory, abstract reasoning, spatial intuition, musical perception, processing speed, learning efficiency — people differ on all of these, for reasons partly innate and partly acquired, and I cannot infer the source from the performance alone. Whatever the source, it changes the cost of learning: some abilities cost one person hundreds of hours of practice and another a short time. Acknowledging this does not deny the dignity of ordinary people, but I refuse to comfort myself with the claim that effort alone can do anything. There are abilities I will probably never beat those people at, and that knowledge produces not only admiration but real anxiety: when a peer of my own age crosses mathematics, physics, music and machine learning without visible strain, I have to think again about the boundary of my own abilities. This is not jealousy. It is the first time I felt, directly, how astonishing the tail of the human distribution can be.

Here the two experiences collide. I met a scientist whose name is already written into the history of AI, and he felt like a flesh-and-blood ordinary person; I know a young man of my own generation with abilities that seem to span everything, and he has no historical name. So how much does a historical database's ranking reflect the true talent of individuals? Astonishingly talented people do not always leave a name in history, and the people who produce great results are not always the best at every cognitive ability of their time. Historical contribution depends on a combination of things: ability and long accumulation, passion and persistence on a specific problem, the direction chosen, the technological window the era happened to open, the institutions and collaboration networks around the person, access to resources and feedback, and luck and unforeseeable events. I am not claiming to know the exact weights, and I am not saying my classmate exceeds LeCun in overall talent; a brief meeting and a few observations cannot rank two human beings. The claim is narrower: historical greatness is not a direct measurement of innate talent. History records the outcomes that actually happened under specific conditions, not all the outcomes that could have happened. A gifted person can miss historical status because of direction, resources, environment or choice, and another can break through on an important problem through persistence and an era's opportunity. Neither fact makes the second un-excellent or the first a failure; the mapping from ability to achievement is complex and uncertain.

My gifted peers do discourage me. My own life goal has become the creation of something extraordinary, and seeing others hold large advantages across several fields makes me doubt whether I have any chance. But if historical achievement is not fully determined by innate talent, I should not predict my entire life from my rank in cognitive ability. I do not need to be the smartest person in every field; I need to find a problem I am willing to invest in for a long time, one where I can keep improving and have a real chance of contributing something that did not exist before. This does not guarantee success: I may fail from insufficient ability, wrong choices, resources, or the era itself. But whether I am worth trying should not be decided by whether I once sat in a classroom with people much smarter than me.

And still the tension must be kept, not dissolved — between wanting a name in history and knowing the odds of getting one. Most people will not become exceptions. Realism can save a person's life, and realism can cage it; idealism built civilizations, and idealism manufactures hallucinations. Sometimes, to do something at all, you have no choice but to run on a placebo effect, because the cruelty of reality can scare you out of doing it. Self-belief beyond the statistics is what lets you move, but it must not blind you to the risk. Respect reality. Just don't worship it.

## Any system should be judged on one scale

None of this is an argument for any particular alternative, and this is where essays like this usually collapse, so let me be careful. Capital accumulation has driven enormous technological progress. Entrepreneurs have created real value. Markets are unmatched at allocating certain kinds of resources. The systems history tried instead had serious problems of their own. UBI, socialism, automation-funded societies: every proposal carries real difficulties, and technology never automatically produces fair institutions. Productivity and distribution are two different problems, and solving the first says nothing about the second. The other -isms beyond capitalism have problems of their own; none of today's systems is the final answer. So my position is not "capitalism is bad". It is narrower: any system should be judged on a single scale — whether it converts productive power into human freedom. GDP measures how much is produced; the question it never answers is what production is for, and who is freed by it. On that scale the current system earns a genuinely mixed verdict. It produces freedom for a few and survival for the many, and the ratio between the two has become the central political question of this century, whether or not we admit we are asking it.

## A structural analysis of socialism and communism

I have been careful so far to aim my criticism at one thing only: any arrangement that fails to convert productive power into human freedom. It would be easy, and wrong, to read that as a coded defense of some other system, so let me say directly what I think about socialism and communism, because the topic deserves more than a sentence.

Start with what socialism is actually for, because it is easy to caricature and I do not want to do that. Socialism is a broad family of ideas about ownership, redistribution, welfare and democratic control of production, and several of its core goals are goals I have already defended in this essay. Social protection: the attempt to partially separate a person's basic needs from their market income. Universal access to education, healthcare and housing when institutions are designed to provide them. Collective risk-sharing, so that one individual does not bear the entire consequences of unemployment, illness, disability, an economic downturn, or a failed experiment at starting a company. The capacity to mobilize resources toward projects with long horizons and no immediate return — exactly the kind of project the market systematically underprices. The prevention of extreme concentrations of private economic power. The idea that some goods are so tied to human dignity that access to them should not depend entirely on purchasing power. And the possibility that greater economic security gives individuals more freedom to study, create, conduct research, change careers, or start companies.

None of these goals requires socialism. Capitalist welfare states, public institutions, taxation, social insurance, cooperatives, public ownership and mixed economies pursue many of the same objectives, and I say this explicitly so that nobody reads this section as "capitalism bad, socialism good". The interesting question is not which label wins. It is which institutional arrangements actually deliver these goods.

Because socialism, examined honestly, also has structural problems of its own, and they are not moral failings of the people who support it. They are institutional. The first is information. Economic knowledge — what people want, what things cost to make, what is possible where — is distributed across millions of individuals and organizations, and no central office can assemble it. The second is incentives: when rewards become weakly connected to contribution, innovation, productivity, risk-taking and responsibility can all decay, without anyone intending it. The third is bureaucracy: organizations created to coordinate resources develop their own interests and drift away from the purpose that created them. The fourth is the soft budget constraint: an organization that keeps receiving resources despite poor performance loses the pressure to improve or to exit. The fifth is the concentration of decision-making: when economic ownership and political authority become highly centralized, economic mistakes become political problems, and whoever controls the plan controls more than the plan. The sixth is the hardest: deciding what should be produced, in what quantity, and according to whose preferences — the very question markets answer automatically and imperfectly. And underneath all of these sits the tension between collective objectives and individual autonomy, plus the uncomfortable fact that replacing private owners of capital with a centralized administrative class does not necessarily eliminate hierarchy. It can merely change who sits at the top of it.

None of this makes socialists stupid, immoral or economically ignorant. It makes central planning difficult, and difficulty is not a moral verdict.

One more distinction gets lost in ordinary argument. Socialism and communism are not interchangeable labels. Socialism, in the sense I am using it, is the broad family: social ownership, redistribution, welfare, democratic control of production. Communism in the classical theoretical sense is a far more radical endpoint: common ownership, the disappearance of class divisions, and, in Marxist theory, the withering away of the state as a coercive political structure. And theoretical communism is not the same thing as the historical political systems that called themselves communist. I will not judge an abstract system only by its idealized description, and I will not judge every theoretical idea only by its worst historical implementation. The gap between institutional ideals and institutional reality is one of the subjects of this essay — it is the same gap as the one between the future we were promised and the future we mock.

That brings the question back to where this essay began. When resources are scarce, telling a person "first find a stable job; survival comes before self-actualization" is completely rational. But suppose AI, robotics, automation, energy technology and advanced manufacturing eventually make many forms of necessary labor extremely cheap. Then the question changes. If a civilization can produce abundant food, housing, transportation, computation and healthcare with relatively little human labor, should access to those things still depend primarily on an individual's ability to sell labor on the market?

This is where the old capitalism-versus-socialism debate becomes interesting again, and it stops being about which ideology is morally superior. It becomes: which institutional arrangements allow technological productivity to translate into greater human freedom?

And two distinctions from earlier must stay sharp. Productivity is not distribution: a society can become extraordinarily productive while the gains from productivity remain highly concentrated. Automation is not liberation: automation can mean humans work less, or work more efficiently, or become unemployed, or watch profits concentrate, or see basic goods get cheaper, or see new forms of employment emerge — or some combination of all of these. Technology changes the feasible set. Institutions determine how much of that feasible set becomes available to ordinary people. So I will not make the naive argument that AI will solve inequality automatically. It will not.

This is where the survival-first realism from earlier returns to view, and with it the three rationalities. Survival rationality is rational under scarcity, and it should not automatically become a theory of what human life is for. A society may reasonably tell a young person "you need an income". But it should also be possible to ask the larger civilizational question: why does access to a meaningful life remain conditional on how successfully you sell your labor? That question sits at the intersection of capitalism, socialism, welfare states, UBI, automation and whatever comes after scarcity — and none of those systems currently answers it well. Modern economic systems are extremely good at answering the first two rationalities — how I stay economically secure, what maximizes my measurable outcome — and much less capable of answering the third, what is actually worth doing. An increasingly productive civilization should eventually give individuals more room to answer the third for themselves. This is not an argument that everyone must become an entrepreneur, an artist, a scientist, or any other kind of extraordinary person; it is the opposite. A good civilization should not require anyone to become extraordinary merely to obtain basic autonomy. An ordinary person should be allowed to remain ordinary without their life being counted as a failure.

Markets are powerful coordination mechanisms, and they can also produce inequality, externalities, monopolies, and situations where purchasing power decides access to necessities. Central planning can mobilize resources and pursue collective objectives, and it faces information, incentive, bureaucratic and power-concentration problems. Welfare states, public ownership, cooperatives, markets, regulation, taxation and social insurance can be combined in many ways. Institutions are technologies, with trade-offs like any other technology. The interesting question is what institutional architecture works best at a given level of technological productivity, information availability, and social complexity.

And the test remains the one I proposed earlier. A civilization should not measure progress only by GDP, corporate profits, stock-market capitalization, employment rates, or productivity per worker. It should also ask: how much freedom does each additional unit of technological productivity give to an ordinary human being? If a hundred robots replace a hundred workers, the most interesting question is not how much GDP increased. It is what happened to the time, autonomy, security, and possibilities of those humans. The ultimate goal of increasing productivity may not be to maximize output at all. It may be to maximize the number of people who no longer have to sell their lives in order to live — and then to leave them alone to decide what living is for.

## A Generation Caught in a Non-War Catastrophe

I want to say something about the generation living through this.

We may be inside a historical catastrophe that involves no war: no artillery, no destroyed cities, no invasion. The data centers keep humming, the AI models keep improving, the tech companies keep minting wealth. And yet for part of a generation, the careers and lives they could have had are being damaged at the root. A catastrophe does not need ruins: long-term unemployment, collapsing incomes, devalued degrees, vanishing career paths, years of effort losing market value — those, too, are catastrophes.

So if you have an excellent degree, a strong record, perhaps real technical or academic achievement, and the only work you can find, in mainland China or the United States, pays two to five dollars an hour, do not immediately conclude that you are incompetent. You may simply have landed in a very unfavorable historical period. Ability and market compensation were never the same variable: cycles, industrial structure, technological substitution, labor supply and demand, geography — all of these decide income. Not every low income is the era's fault; personal choices matter too. I am saying a low wage is not a final verdict on your intelligence, your effort, or your potential. Two to five dollars is the extreme edge of a bad period, not the ordinary legal wage anywhere.

I worry that what is happening now is more complicated than Japan after 1991. The 1991 bubble burst and the stagnation that followed shaped a generation's professional chances. What young people face now may be structurally worse: slowdown, global industrial competition, AI automation, credential devaluation, and a distribution between the returns to capital and to labor that grows more lopsided every year. In some respects the long-term effect on the young may be more severe than Japan's lost decades. This is my judgment and an analogy, not a verified economic conclusion; I am not claiming that today's losses already exceed everything Japan went through. The sentence I actually believe is smaller and colder: a generation can lose its future without ever experiencing a war.

And the cruelest part is the asymmetry of time. An economic crisis can end, a technological shock can be absorbed, new industries can hire. But a human life does not regrow the way an economic indicator does. If the catastrophe lasts five years, a person can lose the starting point of a career. If it lasts twenty, they can lose the most creative period of their life. If the shock follows them for a lifetime, even if society prospers again half a century later, nothing will automatically compensate the opportunities that were taken. They may never know what they could have become in another historical period. I will not promise that everyone eventually recovers, because some of them will not.

If history takes away the future you could have had, you are allowed to ask God why. If a catastrophe you never controlled drags your life into the mud, you have the right to be angry — at fate, at the era, at God, existentially rather than doctrinally. You do not have to pretend any of it is fair in order to look mature or strong. But how you face it — whether you keep creating, whether you keep searching for meaning — is still yours to decide. Nobody has the right to demand that you forgive the era, and I will not tell you that suffering makes you stronger.

Look at the world we are building: does it not resemble a dystopia? In the same civilization, a small number of AI researchers, founders and early equity holders hold stock worth tens or hundreds of millions of dollars, while ordinary workers live on something like five hundred dollars a month — an example of poverty, not a statistic for the average worker anywhere. One end of the system has compute, capital, automation and immense wealth; the other end struggles for basic economic security. This is no complaint about researchers being paid well, or about technology's creators. The question that disturbs me: why does a civilization with this much productive power still leave so many people unable to reach economic security and autonomy over their own lives?

Because civilizational progress and individual catastrophe are not mutually exclusive. A technological revolution can create enormous long-run value while inflicting irreversible short-run losses, over one person's entire career. GDP rising does not mean every income rises. Efficiency improving does not mean every job improves. A society growing richer does not mean the people who bear the cost of the transition share in the gains. A civilization can advance technically while a generation's opportunities shrink; the eventual wealth of the revolution does not erase the suffering of the people it ran over.

I cannot promise that this generation will be compensated, and I cannot promise that I will not end up among the losers of the technological shock. But I refuse to believe that losing economic position in a catastrophe means losing personal worth. A capable young person who finds no opportunity in the wrong historical period has not simply failed as an individual. And that is the same demand as the rest of this essay.

## The Fear of Supporting Humanity

In the ordinary imagination, one hundred million yuan — roughly fourteen million dollars — is an enormous fortune; an average employee could work for decades without coming close. But when I started looking at global technology companies, quantitative trading firms, energy traders and industrial conglomerates, I realized: the economic world is far larger than the part of it that ordinary people can see. A fortune that looks almost infinite from inside one life is a small unit inside the modern capital system. That gap changed how I think about wealth, scarcity and the value of labor.

Take Anthropic. By 2026 it employs thousands of people, and its valuation and equity plans have created paper wealth that ordinary workers find hard to imagine. In November 2026, Reuters reported that its IPO is poised to create thousands of paper millionaires among current and former employees ([Reuters, via The Star](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/11/19/anthropic-ipo-to-mint-thousands-of-millionaires)). I am not claiming every Anthropic employee holds twenty million dollars in equity, and unlisted equity is not cash. The point is the gap: income from labor and returns from capital ownership live on different orders of magnitude. Owning the right part of the right company in the right wave can out-earn decades of highly credentialed professional work — not because one person worked harder or is smarter, but because of ownership structure, timing, organizational position, and capital markets.

A childhood friend of one of my friends now works at Anthropic. That fact changed something in me. Anthropic is no longer only a distant name in financial news, funding reports and AI discussions; it has become a place two degrees of social connection away from my own reality. I have no confirmed direct contact with that employee, and I know nothing about his position, salary, equity or daily work, so I will not invent any of it. What struck me was not his possible wealth, but the contrast between social distance and economic distance. A person inside my friend's social network has entered one of the core companies of the AI revolution, while many people of our generation struggle to find any stable, reasonably paid job. We live in the same era and inside connected social networks, yet the capital, opportunities and technological resources we can reach are utterly different. The distance between two people in a social network can be remarkably small, while the distance between their economic opportunities can be enormous. I am not describing every Anthropic employee as a billionaire, and joining Anthropic does not guarantee financial freedom. The point is simpler: the gigantic differences of the modern capital system suddenly entered my real life through one very concrete human relationship.

The companies that embody this scale are often invisible to consumers. Most people know Apple, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta and Tesla. But the global economy also contains giant firms the wider public has never heard of, and the gap can be especially wide between mainland China's general public and global capital markets. I call this the economic scale–public awareness gap — a personal observation, not a measured survey.

Start with energy and commodity trading. Vitol, the world's largest independent energy trader, reported turnover of about 343 billion dollars in 2025, with net profit reportedly around 4.5 billion ([Vitol](https://www.vitol.com/vitol-2025-volumes-and-review/)). Turnover is not profit: commodity traders move enormous volumes on thin margins. Trafigura's revenue for fiscal 2025 was about 240 billion dollars ([Trafigura](https://www.trafigura.com/news-and-insights/insights/2025-trafigura-annual-results/financial-review/)). Cargill is one of the largest private agricultural and food companies on Earth. A person who knows Netflix and Spotify may never have heard of Vitol, yet Vitol participates in economic activity measured in hundreds of billions a year.

Then the quantitative and market-making world. Citadel is a large multi-strategy hedge fund manager; Citadel Securities, a separate entity, is one of the world's biggest market makers. Jane Street is a giant of quantitative trading and market making. These firms do not need to advertise to the public: scale comes from capital, algorithms, trading infrastructure and financial markets. Many employ relatively few people, so their capital density looks nothing like a labor-intensive company's. Assets under management are neither a company's market value nor a manager's personal wealth.

Then the invisible layer of enterprise software and digital infrastructure. ServiceNow runs enterprise workflows; Constellation Software has built itself by acquiring vertical-market software companies; Equinix operates the data centers and interconnections through which much of the internet physically flows. A consumer uses services carried by this infrastructure every day without learning the names behind it. The global economy is not only the brands the public recognizes.

Then the industrial, semiconductor and supply-chain layer. Synopsys makes the software used to design chips. Amphenol makes connectors and interconnects. McKesson is one of the world's largest distributors of pharmaceuticals and medical supplies. These companies rarely produce products consumers argue about, but they provide the infrastructure the modern world runs on — from chip design software to hospital drug delivery. Some are household names among engineers; the gap is between industry fame and public fame.

And then the new wave. Mercor, which staffs domain experts to train AI models, was valued at about twenty billion dollars in November 2026 ([Staffing Industry Analysts](https://www.staffingindustry.com/news/global-daily-news/mercor-lands-750m-funding-round-valuing-staffing-platform-at-20b)). Databricks runs a massive data and AI platform. Anduril builds autonomous defense systems. Not all of these have the same scale; some are private, some public. But many of these firms are household names within their industries, yet remain almost invisible to the wider public. Our perception of wealth is shaped by consumer visibility, not by the actual structure of the global economy.

The scale turns vertiginous next to a city's economy. By 2026, Elon Musk's net worth was estimated at around one trillion dollars, with some estimates above 1.1 trillion after the SpaceX IPO. Shanghai's GDP in 2025 was about 5.67 trillion yuan, roughly 814 billion dollars ([Xinhua](http://english.scio.gov.cn/pressroom/2026-01/21/content_118292482.html)). One person's paper net worth can reach or exceed the order of magnitude of a megacity's annual output — a stock against a flow, not a rigorous comparison. The point is simply that the scale of contemporary wealth has outrun our intuitions.

This is where Liu Cixin's *Supporting Humanity* (赡养人类) becomes relevant. It imagines a civilization where capital and property have concentrated until a single "ultimate owner" holds nearly all the means of production — a thought experiment about property institutions, not a prediction. But it asks a question that matters more every year: when productivity has grown enough to support an entire civilization, why would most people still lack freedom to obtain basic resources? AI, robotics, energy and manufacturing make that question harder to dismiss as fiction.

And yet when societies discuss UBI, public guarantees, automation dividends or free basic services, the familiar objections arrive immediately. If survival no longer requires labor, who will work? Who pays? Who decides how resources are allocated? Without poverty as a threat, will society keep innovating? These questions contain real economic and institutional difficulties, and I do not pretend otherwise. But they also contain a long-accumulated psychological inertia. Humanity has learned to imagine machines replacing labor, but has not learned to imagine survival without compulsory labor. In a materially scarce society, making survival conditional on labor had a strong basis in reality; as technology lowers the cost of necessary labor, should the institution adjust with it? I call this resistance the fear of supporting humanity. And it does not live only among the rich: ordinary people, too, often feel that unconditional security would be unfair, even a moral defect. We have learned to confirm our social value through labor income, so when machines can do the labor, we no longer know how to redefine the relationship between the right to live and personal value.

One more correction, so this does not turn into a naive redistribution fantasy. A person whose assets are valued at a trillion dollars does not hold a trillion in cash; a valuation cannot directly pay for public healthcare, housing or energy, and a one-time redistribution of a few fortunes cannot sustain humanity indefinitely. Durable security depends on real productive capacity — energy, housing, medical supply, infrastructure, automation — and the institutions that organize them. Capital is a claim on future productivity, not productivity itself. The real question is not "why don't we take all the money from billionaires?" but: why has converting technological productivity into universal human autonomy not become one of civilization's central engineering problems?

Charities, welfare states and basic income experiments exist; I am not claiming indifference. But this civilization pours astonishing resources into advanced AI, Mars, semiconductor fabrication and giant data centers, while making humanity less dependent on selling labor time for basic survival is rarely treated as a goal of equal rank. We can design the most complex AI training systems in history, build infrastructure worth hundreds of billions, study interstellar propulsion — and yet public imagination about universal economic autonomy remains stuck in arguments about fiscal burden, moral hazard and welfare abuse. I am not saying the engineering budgets could be transferred to social security without cost; they could not. I am talking about the order of civilizational priorities and the ambition of institutional innovation, not about moving money from one line to another.

Which returns to the question under everything: what is technological progress ultimately for? If AI keeps raising productivity while lowering the professional value of large numbers of ordinary people, then who exactly is being liberated? If automation keeps creating more wealth while ordinary people must still sell most of their lives for housing, healthcare and basic existence, then the progress of this civilization is incomplete. I do not oppose the creation of enormous wealth, and I do not oppose excellent entrepreneurs, scientists and engineers being paid enormously for it. What I oppose is treating the outcome of the labor market as the final verdict on how much freedom each person deserves. A great civilization should be able to do several things at once: guarantee basic survival and freedom, preserve the incentives for innovation and competition, respect individual autonomy, and allow more and more people to spend their energy on creation, exploration and self-realization.

Perhaps the deepest fear of an advanced civilization is not that machines will become capable of supporting humanity, but that humanity will have to reconsider why survival was ever made conditional on labor.

## The Gradient-Driven Expectation Field

I have tried, more than once, to describe the gap between what technology actually is and what people expect it to become. At some point I stopped writing sentences and tried to write an equation. The result is a crude model I call the gradient-driven expectation field. It is my own exploratory construction, not an empirically validated law, and I will say plainly at the end what it cannot do.

The first assumption is that the actual capability of a technology within one paradigm follows a logistic curve:

$$S(t)=\frac{L}{1+e^{-k(t-t_0)}}$$

\(S(t)\) is the capability at time \(t\), \(L\) the ceiling of this particular paradigm, \(k\) the growth parameter, \(t_0\) the inflection point where growth peaks. \(L\) is not the final limit of human technology; it is the limit of one paradigm, one performance metric, one S-curve.

The growth rate is \(v(t)=S'(t)\), and the relative growth rate is \(\lambda(t)=S'(t)/S(t)\). The relative rate is the thing humans actually feel. The model's core claim is that people extrapolate from it:

$$P(t,\tau)=S(t)e^{\lambda(t)\tau}$$

At time \(t\), people predict the capability at \(t+\tau\) by extending the current relative growth rate exponentially forward. This is bounded rationality: the local slope is treated as if it were a law of the whole future.

But how far forward people dare to extend the trend is not fixed. When growth is explosive, the social imagination becomes more aggressive, so the extrapolation horizon becomes a function of the growth rate:

$$R(t)=R_{\max}\left(\frac{S'(t)}{\max S'(t)}\right)^{\beta}$$

\(R(t)\) is the effective horizon, \(R_{\max}\) the maximum allowed extrapolation, and \(\beta>0\) the sensitivity of the horizon to the growth rate. This \(R(t)\) is an assumption inside the model, not a prediction horizon measured in psychology experiments.

Combining the horizon with the current relative growth rate gives the expectation field:

$$E(t)=S(t)e^{\lambda(t)R(t)}=S(t)\exp\left[R_{\max}\frac{S'(t)^{1+\beta}}{S(t)(\max S'(t))^\beta}\right]$$

\(E(t)\) is the future capability the public expects, expressed on the same capability scale as \(S(t)\) — not a stock price, not a market value, not public satisfaction.

For a single logistic stage, with \(s(t)=S(t)/L\), this reduces to the core form of the model:

$$E(t)=Ls(t)\exp\left[kR_{\max}(1-s(t))\left(4s(t)(1-s(t))\right)^{\beta}\right]$$

The gap between expectation and reality is:

$$B(t)=E(t)-S(t+R(t))$$

When \(B(t)>0\), the public expected more than what arrives; when \(B(t)<0\), it underestimated the technology. \(B(t)\) is an abstract proxy for an expectation bubble — not a real financial bubble, which would also involve capital costs, liquidity, competition, investment incentives and asset pricing.

The most important behavior the model permits is this: \(S'(t)>0\) does not imply \(E'(t)>0\). The technology can continue improving while society becomes increasingly disappointed in it. After a fast-growth phase, the relative growth rate falls, the horizon \(R(t)\) collapses, and the expectations formed during the boom stop being met. The technology is objectively stronger than five years ago, and the public conversation is more pessimistic than five years ago. Nothing in the technology stopped; only the second derivative of the expectation did. I am saying the model allows this dynamic, not that it predicts it for every technology.

A single logistic cannot describe technologies that cross paradigms, so the capability can be written as a sum of stages:

$$S(t)=\sum_i \frac{L_i}{1+e^{-k_i(t-t_i)}}$$

with \(v(t)=S'(t)\), \(R(t)=R_{\max}\left(v(t)/\sup v(t)\right)^{\beta}\), and

$$E(t)=S(t)\exp\left[R_{\max}\frac{v(t)^{1+\beta}}{S(t)(\sup v(t))^\beta}\right]$$

where \(\sup v(t)\) is the supremum of the growth rate over the studied interval. This allows several rounds of breakthrough, expectation inflation and disappointment: theory first, then commercialization, then a new architecture that starts the next cycle. That different stages add up is a modeling assumption, not a claim that every technological revolution obeys logistic curves.

Against the AI present, the model reads naturally. The early capability jumps of large language models, the steady gains attributed to scaling laws, and the arrival of agents, world models and embodied AI each move the perceived growth rate, and with it the horizon \(R(t)\). When a stage improves quickly, society over-extrapolates the short trend into radical expectations about AGI, automation and job displacement; when a route hits constraints of cost, data, compute, reliability or real-world interaction, the expectation falls — while the technology itself keeps improving. A new paradigm can then re-ignite the cycle. The model does not say AI will hit a wall, and it does not say AGI arrives in any particular year. It describes how expectations are formed. It is not an oracle.

Which connects the model to the rest of this essay. Society's imagination of AI, robotics, UBI and post-scarcity civilization is not strictly synchronized with the actual pace of technology. In fast phases the public believes a fabulously rich future is imminent; when growth slows, or the gains are distributed unevenly, or institutions lag behind the technology, the same public swings toward disappointment. Three variables must be separated: technological capability, public expectation, and institutional distribution. My model describes the relationship between the first two. The third is not decided by any of these formulas; it needs its own economic and institutional theory. And that is the essay's central claim in mathematical dress: a civilization may overestimate how quickly technology will transform society, while simultaneously underestimating how profoundly it may eventually change the conditions of human life. The short term gets overpriced and the long term gets underpriced, and between the two sits an institutional problem no gradient can solve.

The limitations, stated plainly. This is a conceptual model with no empirical validation yet. A technology's capability \(S(t)\) is hard to define as a single comparable scalar. Public expectations are driven by media, capital markets, politics, culture and personal experience, not only by the growth rate. The dependence of \(R(t)\) on the growth rate is a hypothesis waiting for data. Real growth can be exponential, power-law, stepwise, logistic, or some combination. The parameters would have to be estimated from real data, not set by intuition, and the existence of an equation does not prove any psychological or sociological law. The model is related to the familiar technology hype cycle, but it tries to give that cycle an explicit dynamical mechanism rather than a slogan. It is not an academically established theory, and I am not presenting it as one.

## Why the old narratives hold on

Why do the old narratives hold on so long after their material base has started to move? Because they are not purely economic. They are built on human nature. On the question of "worldly wisdom" (人情世故): it is actually built on human nature. Over long evolution, humans evolved emotional needs to cope with survival — because of Dunbar's number, our emotional bandwidth is limited to a small circle, so societies end up running on trust, status, and ritual rather than pure reason. 人情世故 is exactly that: an operating system built on top of human nature. And an operating system is not replaced the moment a better one becomes theoretically available; it gets patched. The survival narrative, the ROI logic, the cynicism: they persist not because they are true, but because they are load-bearing. They manage fear, status, family obligations, the need to feel safe. A civilization changes its productive base first and its stories last. The lag is not a bug. It is the emotional firmware of the system. Humanity now has more power to change the world than at any point in its history, and our social psychology may still be living in an age of scarcity.

## No one changes the world alone

One correction against the loneliness of all this. There is actually no one who truly changes the world by the strength of one person alone. Civilization is built by many like-minded people. Those entrepreneurs may claim to be "changing the world," but Musk's plans were actually proposed by many people before him — and if you really want to be pedantic, even Einstein's ideas stood on the shoulders of countless predecessors. The person who refuses to be a statistical average does not become a solitary hero. Exceptions are made of networks, institutions and accidents as much as of will.

## The gravity of reality

As Liu Cixin said, all soaring thoughts will come crashing down under the gravity of reality. I don't read that line as surrender. It is an interface problem: between the ideal and reality there is an interface, and it must be optimized and reconciled, over and over — hold onto the ideal, respect the reality. Most attempts will fail. That is exactly why the few who try matter, and exactly why "don't get above yourself" is such a catastrophic piece of advice: it optimizes the survival of individuals, and taken to its limit it guarantees the stagnation of the species. Humanity may be sitting on the Great Filter, but the bugs have never been wiped out — perhaps a black swan event will lead humanity step by step out of the swamp.

## Conclusion

I know reality is cruel. I know most people fail. I know resources are distributed extremely unequally. I know ability and luck matter. I know "survive first" is often the correct advice. I know Homejoy proves that ideals must submit to reality's tests. I know cynicism is attractive because it is so often statistically correct. I know placebo-style confidence cannot change the probabilities.

But reality is not an oracle. Probability is not fate. A survival strategy is not the meaning of a life. Employment is not the purpose of a civilization. Growing productivity should not only mean working more efficiently; it should mean that fewer and fewer people must work merely to live. A civilization should ultimately be judged by whether it gives human beings more and more ability to decide what they live for.

Respect reality, but don't worship it. Reality should constrain our plans, not define the full boundary of our imagination. Statistics describe the majority; they do not dictate the destiny of an individual. And no one should have to become extraordinary just to deserve the right to choose their own life.

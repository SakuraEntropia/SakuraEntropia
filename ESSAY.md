# Preface: Some Thoughts on Humans and Existing Social Systems

*A living essay. First published August 2026. Last updated November 20, 2026.*

## The promised future

We were once promised a future of uncompromised abundance: humanity reaching UBI, commuting in eVTOLs, kilometer-scale megastructures, ring-shaped space stations, autonomous robots running household logistics, all grueling physical toil offloaded to machinery, and resources allocated directly on demand.

Post that vision online today, and you will be met with immediate, acidic mockery: "Keep dreaming — anything exists in dreams." That is the predictable collision when an ideal crashes into the messy reality of productivity and chaotic systems; modern societal machinery is still ruled by primal human emotion. Yet that mockery defends a way of living far more than it admits — it is not merely a cynical verdict on the trajectory of the world, but an alibi for a compromised, submissive existence.

We have engineered machines that fly, compute, translate, paint, and diagnose. Standing on that level of productive capacity, a civilization should have the confidence to interrogate questions far grander than "how do I scramble to survive another month?" Yet absurdly, almost everyone I know — including myself on most days — organizes their entire existence around that single, frantic question. Why does a civilization with a clear path toward abundance still demand that almost every human being auction off their life's waking hours just to earn permission to exist? Why, when technology steadily reduces the total quantum of labor required for survival, does culture relentlessly shove everyone down the same lockstep track: study, get hired, take on a mortgage, marry, reproduce, and grind toward retirement? Beneath both questions lies a colder reality: is realism helping people adapt to the world, or is it dressing up the status quo as the only imaginable reality? This essay is my attempt to dissect that chain of constraints, without pretending I have arrived at a neat solution.

## What a great civilization means

My definition of a Type I civilization is concrete: the capacity to travel anywhere on this planet, to reshape planetary geography as needed, to make round trips to Mars routine, and to eradicate nearly every fatal disease. On this scale, progress is no sentimental slogan; it is an engineering checklist, and we are already ticking the boxes. The Eurasian Pole of Inaccessibility once defeated the ancients; today, right next to it, stands Tianshan International Airport — a 4F-class facility. Shenzhen was once a malaria-ridden, cockroach-infested marsh; no ancient seer could have foretold its rise into a nerve center of global computing. To truly qualify as an exceptional civilization, we at least need an elevator running up Mount Everest, and the capability to ferry ordinary citizens into Lop Nur and the depths of the Amazon — anywhere on Earth. Anyone should be able to touch the red dirt of Mars. Musk and founders like him are battering down that door, even as they smash headlong into the viscous friction of the existing bureaucratic apparatus.

There is a glaring fracture here: on one side stands what humanity can pull off as a collective species; on the other, the suffocating boundary of what an individual is permitted to desire. We can pour enough high-grade concrete at the pole of inaccessibility to land jumbo jets on a 4F runway, yet we still turn around and lecture any ambitious young person: hunt down a stable job, take on thirty years of mortgage debt, scramble for a safe civil-service desk. Civilization races toward the stars, while the humans inside it are ordered to stay frozen in place. That fracture is what everything here is really about.

## The ordinary person

"Ordinary person" is a term blurred by cultural convenience. In a sense, anyone can be called ordinary outside their wheelhouse: Musk dominates commercial and industrial engineering, but placed on an athletic field he might look completely lost. My operational definition is much narrower: a middling employee at a conventional company, earning around the median wage, living paycheck to paycheck and forced to sell physical or mental labor continuously just to keep breathing, shuffling along the prescribed track of schooling, marriage, children, and retirement, with zero plans for entrepreneurship or breakthrough self-actualization. Measurably: a person whose asset income is near zero, whose bargaining power is thinner than a single layoff shock, and who would fall below the subsistence line if their wages stopped for three months.

In contemporary discourse, "ordinary" has metastasized into something akin to a modern Dalit caste — an indelible brand that stamps an individual as trapped for life. During prolonged economic freezes, most people are indeed starved of opportunity, chained to debt, and grind through existence on that fixed rail.

Yet ordinary never means worthless. Someone who never founds a unicorn, breaks new scientific ground, produces enduring art, stands on an Olympic podium, or commands a public following has not failed at being human. The tragedy of modern civilization is not that the majority remain ordinary; it is that "ordinary" has been weaponized into an unbreachable caste. I have no faith in, nor any desire for, a utopian madhouse where everyone is coerced into being a prodigy. A genuinely humane society must never demand that someone become a genius before granting them the right to steer their own life. The target is never "everyone must be extraordinary" — it is that no one should have to become extraordinary just to earn basic autonomy.

## Distribution

Why is the average person's path locked into this groove? The root cause is a radically skewed resource distribution. As of 2026, human society still runs on the underlying source code of capitalism. This is no moral melodrama of saints versus villains — it is a structural distribution mechanism, tilted by design. The vast majority own zero means of production, so they must sell their labor time to stay alive; and the paycheck they receive is calculated to cover barely the reproduction of that labor: rent, crushing commutes, baseline calories. That is precisely why those three expenses swallow most of an income. Nobody needs to hatch backroom conspiracies against you — the machinery only requires that you show up on time; it never asks you to excel. The surplus your sweat produces compounds into capital, clustering at blinding speed into fewer and fewer hands. That concentrated capital dictates what gets funded, deciding who gets the material runway to swing at greatness. A breakthrough in any hard domain today routinely costs millions — a lifetime's net earnings for an average worker. For most people, the gate is bolted shut before they even reach the handle. Entrepreneurs are not comic-book villains; they are merely players operating inside the system with accumulated surplus. But the structure itself is guilty: it turns society's collective surplus into a tollbooth only a tiny elite can pass. As long as surplus flows along capital accumulation, how far most people can travel is decided by that distribution — and right now, that distribution mocks human effort.

Let me state the statistical wager plainly: this essay argues K-shaped divergence, not a shift in the mean. Under a K-shape the middle hollows out while both tails thicken — and that is precisely when the median stops being informative, because it reports on the segment that is disappearing. Using the median to refute the extremes is like using the average temperature to prove glaciers do not exist. The extremes are not noise; they are the object of study. What actually needs proving is the thinning of the middle, and that evidence can only come from the shape of the distribution, never from a single measure of central tendency.

Why don't more people launch startups or fight for excellence? First, their financial cushion cannot withstand a single tremor. Second, downside failure is lethal. Third, the surrounding social apparatus conditions them into obedience around the clock. Fourth, their cognitive and skill moats may not yet have cleared the bar. These are not excuses; they are the physical mechanics of the trap.

## The pyramid of needs

Material limits draw the boundary, but external constraints alone cannot explain cultural inertia. Beneath those constraints sits a suppressed hierarchy of human needs.

Maslow's pyramid of motivation may look too neat for real life, but as a map of the human predicament, it strikes true. A person whose baseline survival is under constant threat cannot be free: free to gamble years on an unproven venture, chase fundamental science, create art, or wander through intellectual frontiers. Without a dependable safety net, every single one of those pursuits is strictly an unaffordable luxury. Prioritizing survival is not a moral failing. For an ordinary person trapped in scarcity, hard-nosed realism is the smartest survival strategy available, and I would never sneer at that pragmatism.

Now shift the question to the civilizational scale. When automated factories, synthetic agriculture, cheap clean energy, universal healthcare, and machine intelligence drive material output into abundance, the sheer labor required to stay alive should stop monopolizing a human life. The civilizational trajectory must pivot from "how do we keep people breathing" to "what can people create once survival is secured." That is the upward movement the pyramid charts: from physical survival and safety to belonging, esteem, and self-actualization. Technological progress is never just about optimizing efficiency metrics on a dashboard; it is about pulling people out of the mud. Freedom here means autonomy — the genuine capacity to author your own existence — not merely the passive absence of interference. Economic security provides the physical muscle for autonomy. A civilization proves its wealth not by the inventory of consumer goods it stacks up, but by the proportion of human beings who no longer have to auction off their lives just to survive.

## Two stories about a human life

Every human society pitches two competing scripts to its members. The first is the survival narrative: get into a decent school, land a safe job, pinch pennies, service a mortgage, marry, raise children, support aging parents, and hold on until retirement. This script answers one urgent question — how a fragile individual survives safely inside the current machinery. It isn't wrong. Across most of human history, through eras of brutal scarcity, it was the only script the material base could sustain.

The second is the civilization narrative: why do we pour generations of intellect into advancing technology? Why automate, train frontier models, build humanoid robotics, chase fusion power, and construct interplanetary craft? What bondage was civilization supposed to break? Hannah Arendt dissected this with surgical precision in *The Human Condition*: labor, the endless cyclical toil to sustain biological life; work, the fabrication of a durable human-made world; and action, the capacity to initiate something unprecedented among peers, leaving an indelible mark ([SEP, *Hannah Arendt*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/arendt/)). A civilization that chains its entire population to the millstone of labor has forgotten what it set out to escape. The civilization narrative exists to compress the survival narrative down to a minimum. If centuries of industrial engines, semiconductors, compute grids, and intelligent robotics culminate in nothing grander than "congratulations — you can now hunt for a job more efficiently," then we have squandered the core promise of the productivity revolution. An abundant civilization has to answer what living is for, not just how to prolong it. As machines shoulder the burden of sustaining society, why should selling your life remain the mandatory toll for permission to exist? I have no clean answer. I simply refuse to accept the current employment contract as a law of physics. It is an arbitrary choice, and the chasm between those two narratives is the storm center of modern exhaustion.

## The ROI of a life

Wedged between these two narratives sits a pervasive formula. Pick a major by its placement rate; evaluate hobbies by side-hustle monetization; judge study by ROI; fund research only if it yields quick patents; pitch startups solely for valuation multiples; price art strictly by auction numbers. A living human being, compressed into an optimization algorithm solving for maximum ROI.

Inside scarcity, this calculation is ruthlessly rational. If a family has no cushion, no reserve capital, zero margin for error, and zero dependable safety nets for healthcare or housing, prioritizing immediate cash flow isn't cowardice — it is cold arithmetic. The madness starts when productivity has already crushed absolute scarcity, yet that desperate survival calculation remains crowned as life's supreme compass anyway.

Three distinct layers make sense here. The first is survival rationality: "I must make money or end up on the street." That is a physical constraint, not a philosophy. The second is instrumental rationality: "Everything I do must justify its direct economic yield." That is the logic of the market colonizing the psyche — an external leash embraced as an identity. Weber drew a parallel line a century ago: instrumental rationality (Zweckrationalität), where every move serves utilitarian ends, set against value rationality (Wertrationalität), where action is pursued for its intrinsic worth, regardless of the cost ([SEP, *Max Weber*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weber/)). My three-layer ladder builds on Weber's concepts. The third layer is that value rationality: things that remain profoundly vital even if they never convert into a dime of profit — basic theoretical exploration, uncompromised art, philosophy, expeditions into uncharted territory, games, wild personal creation, pure curiosity, and open public knowledge gifted to humanity.

Once instrumental rationality becomes the only legal dialect, alienation is complete. "Why study mathematics?" — "Because quantitative finance pays seven figures," never "because I want to grasp mathematics itself." "Why make art?" — "Because commercial illustration monetizes," not because there is an unbearable urge to create. The market's balance sheet has colonised the self. The real question is when a civilization ever becomes wealthy enough to let ordinary people ask "what is worth dedicating a life to," instead of whipping them to calculate "what yields the fastest return." That isn't an economics query; it is a confrontation over what an economy is actually for.

## The Zhang Xuefeng-style narrative

In contemporary China, this survival-first mindset has an unmistakable public face. Zhang Xuefeng is simply the most visible brand in an army of admissions and career coaches; I have no interest in judging the man, but I want to dissect the doctrine. Call it the Zhang Xuefeng-style narrative: drop airy ideals, lock onto placement metrics, paychecks, tier-one cities, safe majors, civil-service tenure (编制), iron-rice-bowl stability, and family downside. Its operational rule is blunt: passions wait, survival comes first.

The popularity of this doctrine rests on brutal realities that command respect: crushing academic marathons, sky-high real estate leverage, cutthroat sorting that begins in the nursery, a cold job market, families with paper-thin margins, terror of downward mobility, a downside so steep that one misstep wipes out three generations of savings, young people with zero runway to fail, parents frantically trying to shield their kids from risk, and credentials fused to survival. In a low-welfare environment with fragile safety nets and exorbitant living costs, "secure a breadwinning post first" is seasoned, protective advice. If I were on that stage advising ordinary households, I would likely give the same counsel.

The poison creeps in unnoticed: a tactical survival move slides into a total philosophy of life. "Secure your bread first" warps into "your life was only ever meant to be a meal ticket." Those are completely different propositions. The first is a logistical recommendation on sequencing under harsh constraints; the second is a verdict on what human existence amounts to.

## Homejoy and the limits of realism

Testing this against a textbook business wreckage cuts to the core, because in front of corporate ruins, realism always sounds most devastating. Homejoy was a YC star in on-demand home cleaning — I watched its founder share the stage with Sam Altman. It grew at runaway speed, pulled in millions in venture capital, drew massive attention, and then abruptly collapsed in 2015. It is routinely wheeled out as an object lesson in cold reality. The market's teeth were real and sharp: churned customers, razor-thin margins, worker-classification lawsuits, rivals at every flank, acquisition costs burning faster than lifetime value, unit economics that refused to close, and burn rates outrunning the story. A founder turning a blind eye to that checklist isn't an idealist; they are simply deaf to feedback.

Homejoy answers a critical question: when must one yield to reality? Tactically, on every single operational move, every second of the day. Realism is no enemy to ambition; it is the raw concrete of it. Ideals without reality's cold feedback decay into hallucinations. But the converse cuts just as deep: reality has no business drawing the final boundary on what can be imagined. Strip out the ideal, and reality becomes a tomb. I use this case because it can serve, inside that YC course, as the direct control group for the 2014 lecture; that does not make it a good example. As a sample it is poor — a company that burned out on unit economics proves neither that market law must always win, nor that ambition should not be tried. Its value is contrast, not exemplar. Homejoy died because its balance sheet didn't work, and that was the market passing a just sentence on a flawed business. The mistake is weaponizing that autopsy: deducing that no one should ever swing at anything hard, sneering that every bold design is just another Homejoy waiting to implode. A business went under; the question of what an enterprise can build remains unblemished.

### The scales of reality

Sam Altman’s trajectory stages a revealing contrast. Across his 2014 Stanford lectures on early YC dogma, the perimeter of reality was deliberately tight: isolate a problem, build an MVP, get users, keep growing, don't run out of money, build a company. That is reality framed for an individual founder. Today, the same man commands an outfit whose bets clash directly with AGI, planetary compute and energy grids, basic research, geopolitics, and civilizational momentum. That isn't hypocrisy; it reveals that reality operates at distinct scales. For an undergrad, reality is "land an offer"; for a founder, reality is "keep the runway alive"; for a civilization, reality is whether power, computation, automation, medicine, and rocketry can shatter the physical boundary of the possible. Every tier runs its own valid realism. The catastrophe is locking down one scale's survival playbook and crowning it the universal metric of truth. When a culture permits people to debate only how to scavenge within established cages, outlawing any question of redesigning the bars, it has gone intellectually bankrupt at the civilizational scale.

This strips the varnish off the warning: 不要自命不凡, "don't get above yourself." A young kid declares, "I'm going to build a company that changes the world," and the room hears pathological arrogance. Two decades later, the same person steers an industrial empire, lecturing on how AI and fusion will rewrite society — and the room applauds his visionary brilliance. Was the sin ever arrogance, or simply that the bet hadn't cashed yet? Watch the taxonomy rotate around the exact same obsession. Before victory: arrogant, unrealistic, madman. After victory: visionary, bold, historic leader. The world rarely objects to ambition; it objects to unverified ambition. "Don't get above yourself" does possess an empirical foundation: most who consider themselves outliers are flatly mistaken. But a statistical baseline does not grant a mandate to decree "never attempt the outlier move." One is a probability; the other is social policing. The gap between them is precisely where cultural coercion does its dirty work.

## Cynicism

Underneath this compliance runs a thick sludge of cynicism. Not standard gloom, but something colder: recognizing that the world is brutal, and mistaking surrender for maturity. "Your startup will almost certainly die." "Your odds of becoming a top scientist are zero." "Statistically, you are generic fodder." "The world isn't fair." "Ideals don't pay rent." Much of this is undeniable. That is precisely why cynicism is toxic: it gets the diagnostic facts entirely right, and then botches the prescription entirely.

The derivation is broken. Most people fail; therefore you shouldn't try. The second line does not follow from the first. "The crowd fails" is an empirical observation; it does not contain the mandate "therefore pack it in." That conclusion is smuggled in under cover of the data. Statistics chart aggregate drifts; they hold zero title over the fate of an individual. And the reality that most gambits collapse does not make the act of gambiting irrational.

This exposes the collision between statistical rationality and individual rationality. If a person calibrates every decision to aggregate probabilities, they are guaranteed never to become an outlier — because every single exception, prior to becoming one, executed moves that violated the population-level optimum. World-class creators repeatedly did what the regression line forbade. Projecting aggregate mechanics onto the individual is circular: it manufactures the very mediocrity it forecast. A civilization where every actor hedges into the safest bunker will never trigger a paradigm shift; it will merely polish existing rails. Statistics can display the risk profile; it cannot dictate value.

None of this is motivational drivel. Most startups do go to zero. Most research efforts vanish without a trace. Luck is brutally decisive. Discrepancies in raw talent and capital reserves are viciously real. Willpower does not warp probability distributions. Resisting cynicism is not delusion that victory is guaranteed; it is knowing the odds are stacked against you and still refusing to amputate your horizons in advance. Camus looked into that same abyss without tendering his resignation; one must imagine Sisyphus happy, he wrote — the clean antithesis of cynicism: revolt without the narcotic of guaranteed victory, and without the cowardly excuse of despair ([SEP, *Albert Camus*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/camus/)).

## Don't get above yourself

Here the crosshairs settle directly on the individual. A young person steps up: "I want to build a company." "I want to crack world-class science." "I want to make art." "I want to rewrite an industry." "I want to produce something that never existed." The room fires back with statistical flak: "Who do you think you are?" "Do you know how many died trying?" "Do you have any idea how low the odds are?" "Get a regular job." "Wake up." The muzzle bears an ancient brand: 不要自命不凡 — don't get above yourself.

The sleight of hand is crude: leveraging low win rates to deny the right to step onto the field. "Most fail" is not equivalent to "this specific individual will fail." "Thousands have tried" is not "you cannot strike a novel synthesis." And "becoming a global titan is nearly impossible" does not contradict "claiming a world-first in a surgically defined space can be shockingly accessible."

### World first

The real numbers mock the old refrain. Bagging Everest demands lethal physical hazards, exorbitant permits, gear fees, and pileups of failed expeditions — yet over 11,000 different climbers have stood on that summit. Tally up every professional athlete, director, game creator, esports champion, icon, singer, founder, mathematician, statesman, physicist, specialist, composer, painter, and sculptor, and the headcount swells. Slice "world-first" narrowly enough, and claiming one ceases to be unthinkable: the first human to run tensor ops through an audio EQ rack; the first person to train a JEPA inside Minecraft — you just execute. The cases sound like punchlines, but the underlying mechanics are rock-solid: human knowledge space is infinite, and whenever you pin down a sufficiently bespoke problem, fresh frontiers fracture open. Grabbing a world-first inside an ultra-targeted niche isn't black magic; the brutal part is formulating the question. Genuine creation isn't climbing someone else’s leaderboard — it is driving down a brand-new coordinate axis. And once I framed it that way, I wanted to see how many people across human history ever hammered down an axis that didn't erode.

## Counting the people who changed the world

At some point I tried counting this directly, cross-referencing historical biographical databases, Wikipedia, and estimates of the cumulative human population. I went through scientists, mathematicians, inventors, engineers, founders, industrialists, writers, cartoonists, directors, animators, fine artists, musicians, athletes, esports competitors, tournament champions, statesmen, explorers, and anyone else who ever left a dent in the historical record. Grouped together, they form a surprisingly massive roster — but stacked against the tens or hundreds of billions of human beings who have ever drawn breath, they amount to almost zero. On that scale of total human lives, genuine achievement is scarce to the point of absurdity.

My primary tool was the Pantheon project. [Pantheon 1.0](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata201575) (Yu et al., 2016) is a manually verified dataset of globally famous biographies: 11,341 individuals with profiles across at least 25 Wikipedia language editions, indexed via language reach and pageviews. [Pantheon 2.0](https://pantheon.world/data/datasets), in its 2025 public release, holds roughly 126,582 records; its Historical Popularity Index (HPI) tracks cross-linguistic cultural fame, not an individual’s physical contribution to human civilization. A separate study, [A cross-verified database of notable people, 3500BC–2018AD](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-022-01369-4) (Laouenan et al., 2022), compiled about 2.29 million cross-verified figures and compared them against cumulative historical population estimates. That was an entirely separate enterprise from Pantheon, and the datasets cannot be conflated under a common metric.

Digging into these datasets reveals massive statistical skew. The core baseline: being remembered is fundamentally distinct from having changed the world. First, fame bias: people remembered through statecraft, mass entertainment, sports, or algorithmic virality did not necessarily deliver a proportionate contribution; an anonymous engineer who rebuilt an industrial pillar will routinely be eclipsed in visibility by a pop star. Second, structural underrepresentation: vast swaths of ancient humanity left zero paper trail, and crucial breakthroughs were forged by actors history never named; the surviving record is heavily skewed by language, geography, social class, gender, and epoch, ensuring datasets miss crowds of critical contributors. Third, modern documentation inflation: media saturation, publishing, and digital databases preserve vastly more contemporary names, but raw searchability confers zero historical weight. Fourth, metric and definition bias: language counts and pageviews are noisy proxies; the HPI can track cross-cultural renown, but it cannot measure causal civilizational impact, and inclusion bars vary wildly across databases. Finally, brutal survivorship bias: we see only the winners who succeeded, got archived, and survived historical attrition; those possessing equal brilliance and grit who were crushed by war, grinding poverty, institutional blockades, temporal ceilings, or sheer bad luck leave no trace. These registries catalog not just ability, but privilege, access, and historical contingency.

So I ran my own rough correction. Treating every registered celebrity as a mover of history is a massive overcount; restricting the bar to "outstanding, concrete, enduring contributions to science, technology, economics, the arts, culture, or societal structure" shears off a huge percentage of those records. At the same time, missing archives mean armies of consequential figures never made the index at all. The adjustment has to cut both ways: strip out empty prestige, recover forgotten architects, and rigorously decouple foundational contributions from conventional career success. My own exploratory estimate, balancing the inflation of mere celebrity against the erasure of uncredited contributors, puts the number of consequential movers across all human history at roughly 200,000 to 250,000 people. This is neither Pantheon’s conclusion nor a rigorously validated statistical model; it is my own exploratory estimate across conflicting datasets and bias corrections. Historical consequence has no sharp natural boundary. I am isolating historical contribution in the narrowest functional sense: not human dignity, not the worth of a life, and not professional status.

When I looked at those numbers, I could not help feeling that this world was almost a kind of hell: even the people famous enough to leave a significant mark on human history seemed to exist in overwhelming numbers, an endless multitude of extraordinary lives against which my own ambitions felt painfully small.

And yet, in the very same civilization, countless unemployed or underemployed young people were struggling to survive on miserable wages, powerless before economic forces they could barely understand or influence, unable to secure even the modest dignity of a decent job—let alone the freedom to pursue something extraordinary.

What these numbers did to me psychologically is hard to overstate. When the sterile trope "most people never leave a mark on history" solidified into concrete quantities, the impact was crushing, close to pure despair. I found myself revolted by the pre-scripted conveyor belt: enter university, graduate, take a desk job at a generic company, sell decades of labor time by the hour, retire, and vanish from collective memory exactly like the billions before me. That life is no crime, but I hold myself to a different demand: before swinging with everything I have to build something truly unique, I refuse to preemptively accept that I am only ever meant to be statistical filler. Three layers matter here. First, Autonomy: economic self-sufficiency, untethered from lifelong dependency on selling hours to an institution. Second, Creation: building artifacts of authentic scientific, technical, artistic, or societal value. Third, Historical legacy: hammering out work durable enough to bend future generations and anchor one's name in history. They intersect without being identical, and I openly admit that the third holds an intense, obsessive grip on me. I know historical preservation turns on luck, distribution channels, and the retroactive whims of later eras — variables entirely out of any individual’s hands. That collision between cold intellectual realism and raw personal appetite is the core driver of my existential dread.

A sharp paradox emerges here. As global population, university systems, institutional science, the internet, and industrial complexes explode, the raw headcount of brilliant minds producing serious work is likely expanding. Concurrently, the frontiers of mature fields have retreated so deep that pushing them forward demands ingesting mountains of literature, burning massive capital, deploying extreme hardware, and coordinating vast organizational engines. A young mind who might have struck gold alone decades ago now collides with entrenched paradigms and heavily armed incumbents. Yet opportunity structures fracture along field lines and the lifecycle of each technological cycle: fresh breakthroughs routinely obliterate old moats and unearth clean territory. Historical probabilities, current competition barriers, and tomorrow’s unexplored spaces are entirely distinct variables that cannot be collapsed together.

This drops me back into my core position. A sane civilization must never demand that someone become extraordinary before granting them dignity, freedom, and a secure baseline; a standard employee deserves the absolute right to run their life as they see fit. Yet for my own skin, I refuse to resign myself to lifelong mediocrity. Society must allow the ordinary to remain ordinary in peace, while defending the right of those obsessed with the extraordinary to pursue it aggressively. There is zero contradiction here: the first is my demand on the institutional order; the second is my demand on myself. I despise a culture that worships only winners, just as I despise using population averages to tell young people they are fools for trying to break out. Even if I end up flat on my face, the attempt itself was not empty air — yet I will not romanticize failure, nor preach that raw effort guarantees redemption: work must submit to the reality check, and every wager has to account for downside risks. Statistical rationality tracks the herd; individual rationality drives the strike. Survival rationality locks down security; value rationality decides what is worth the blood. Freedom is a basic right; achievement is a high-stakes gamble. Respect reality, but don't worship it.

I met Yann LeCun in person at a public lecture at Nanyang Technological University in Singapore. He is a titan of modern deep learning and a Turing Award laureate. Prior to that afternoon, he was just an untouchable name across papers and tech headlines, living in a realm ordinary mortals don't cross. Then I sat in the same auditorium, got the floor to ask a question, and exchanged a few words. He radiated zero mythological aura. What hit me instead was an irreverent, unvarnished realization: he was simply a very, very lucky human being. Calling him an ordinary human is no swipe at his breakthrough research; it means that when an intellectual giant steps off the pedestal and stands in front of you, you confront a physical person. He was not born with a reservation for the Turing Award; he was once a young researcher fumbling through a dark landscape where no one knew where computing was heading. His standing was built on real ability, decades of stubborn persistence, calculated bets, collaboration, and the unique technical conditions of a specific era. And running straight through it was luck that cannot be brushed aside: his chosen research bets happened to become bedrock pillars of modern AI, whereas history offers zero guarantees that every mind with equivalent drive and talent will ever intersect that kind of temporal tailwind. A brief exchange in an auditorium does not decipher decades of scientific grind, nor am I reducing monumental contributions to pure dumb luck — it was simply the visceral shock of watching a myth resolve into a human face.

That encounter contrasted sharply with another experience that battered my worldview from the opposite flank. I knew a student in a neighboring department. He had made his discipline's national olympiad team, mastered university physics and music theory, earned a grade ten certification in vocal performance, and possessed absolute pitch. When he leaned into our mathematics department's curriculum, problems that cost me hours of grueling labor were absorbed by him as effortlessly as drawing breath. He was simultaneously embedded in two elite labs — one in materials science, the other in machine learning — and I heard a professor call him one of the sharpest minds he had ever mentored. I don't need to invent his credentials or inflate him into a superhero; this was simply the raw observation of a classmate. What it forced me to swallow was jagged and undeniable: certain individuals wield a cross-disciplinary cognitive bandwidth that defies comparison. Once you watch that operate up close, the comfortable egalitarian fairy tale — that all human cognitive hardware starts at parity, and identical sweat generates identical outputs — collapses completely.

Raw talent is real, and the card distribution across humanity is merciless. Working memory, abstract deduction, spatial orientation, pitch discrimination, cognitive processing speed, learning velocity — humans vary wildly across every parameter. Whether rooted in biology or early developmental luck, talent dictates the marginal cost of mastery: an intellectual threshold demanding hundreds of hours of grinding torture from one person is cleared by another in an afternoon. Acknowledging this does not strip dignity from ordinary lives, but I refuse to drug myself with the cheap slogan that brute persistence can conquer anything. In certain arenas, I will likely never touch the taillights of people like that. The realization generates more than intellectual respect; it breeds authentic, marrow-deep dread. Watching a peer glide effortlessly across abstract mathematics, physics, music theory, and deep learning forces a violent confrontation with the limits of my own machine. That isn't petty jealousy. It is the raw shock of colliding head-on with the extreme right tail of the human capability distribution.

Those two encounters crash together violently. On one hand, a monumental figure etched into the bedrock of AI history, revealing himself in person as a concrete human carried by historic timing; on the other, a peer whose cognitive machinery completely outclasses everyone in sight, holding zero historical fame. How reliably, then, do biographical indices reflect raw individual genius? Astounding talent does not guarantee historical resonance, and the people who land era-defining breakthroughs are rarely the absolute smartest across every cognitive vector. Real civilizational contribution is never a linear projection of innate intellect; it is a tangled probabilistic matrix: core capability fused with relentless accumulation, irrational persistence on a specific frontier, strategic direction, the historical window cracking open at the exact moment, institutional scaffolding and network leverage, access to compute and capital feedback loops, and raw stochastic contingency. A handful of hallway observations cannot rank two human beings. The actual claim is tight: historical greatness is not an unmediated benchmark of innate horsepower. History archives only what concretely manifests under specific constraints; it awards zero medals to latent potential that starved in the dark. An astonishing intellect can vanish into historical silence through bad timing, broken runway, hostile environments, or mistaken bets, while another mind, locked onto the right fracture point when the structural window swings open, hammers home a historic breakthrough. Neither dynamic diminishes the victor nor reduces the lost to failure; the transfer function from biological capability to historical consequence is nonlinear, turbulent, and unforgiving.

Peers with that level of raw talent do break you over their knee. I have staked my existence on forging something truly extraordinary, and watching someone casually command insurmountable moats across multiple frontiers induces brutal self-doubt: what right do I have to believe I have a shot? Yet if historical consequence is not a direct mirror of biological gifts, I have no reason to forecast my entire trajectory from a raw cognitive percentile. I don't need to be the sharpest intellect in every room I walk into; I need to unearth an uncompromising problem that I am willing to hammer at relentlessly, where I can build compound momentum, and where a genuine crack exists to build something that never stood before. This offers zero promise of victory: I may still crash hard from capped ability, disastrous choices, dry runways, or the sheer indifference of the era. But whether the fight is worth the candle will never be determined by whether I once sat in a lecture hall next to a mind ten times sharper than mine.

I keep circling back to Lacan’s thesis in his 1946 "Presentation on Psychical Causality": if a man who thinks he is a king is mad, a king who thinks he is a king is no less so ([Lacan, *Écrits*](https://lacanianworks.org/presentation-on-psychical-causality-28th-september-1946-bonneval-hospital-paris)). The first lunatic misjudges his coordinate in the world; the second misjudges what the coordinate actually is. A monarch occupies the throne because institutions, laws, symbolic systems, and the collective recognition of others hold him there. The seat is functional and its coercive power is lethal, but none of it constitutes the ontological essence of the human animal occupying the chair. Believing that "king" is your innate, indivisible selfhood is just a deeper, socially sanctioned madness. I use Lacan’s framing here to target that catastrophic trap of excessive symbolic identification. The intellectual foundation rests on Hegel: self-consciousness achieves actualization only through the recognition (Anerkennung) of the other ([SEP, *Recognition*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/recognition/)), which is precisely what makes the paradox bite so deep.

When I flip that lens onto myself, the reflection is disturbing. I am terrified of living, rotting, and dying as an interchangeable statistic. I comb through Pantheon, obsessively tallying how many human beings ever anchored their names in the stone of history. I want to build as a scientist, a creator, a founder, a figure whose work alters the timeline. But what if my hunger for the extraordinary is itself just another blind loop of symbolic identification? If I require validation from the historical ledger to justify my right to draw breath, I have already outsourced my existence to a social rank. Suppose I succeed — suppose I build the enterprise, publish the breakthroughs, and force my way into the Pantheon index. Suppose I then internalize the title "great man" as if the abstraction were my actual flesh. Wouldn't I become the king who believes he is a king? The trap isn't the ambition itself, nor acknowledging hard competence; the poison is confusing an external title — built out of historical luck, institutions, and the applause of others — with the baseline truth of who you are.

Seeing LeCun across that auditorium brought the abstraction down into physical reality. The face that previously existed only in breakthrough preprints and Silicon Valley mythology was, underneath the credentials, a flesh-and-blood human being. Turing laureate, father of convolutional networks, historic architect — none of those badges exhausted his person, and he didn't spend the evening posing as a bronze monument. The recurring blunder we make around monumental figures is forgetting that before they were forged into symbols, they were just people trying to figure things out. A brief glimpse doesn't grant me ownership of his life’s journey, but it hammered home a vital truth: every symbol has a human face.

A classmate once confronted me with a blunt scenario: if I placed a once-in-a-century polymath right in front of you — someone who comprehensively torches you in animation, computer graphics, and every single domain you obsess over — would you just throw in the towel and drop dead? I hear that question with different ears now. A genius who crushes me in those arenas crushes me in those arenas; his brilliance does not invalidate my right to stand on the earth. If my self-worth is entirely chained to my rank on an arbitrary leaderboard, the leaderboard owns me regardless of where I land. Even sitting at number one, you live in terror of number two. It is a suffocating form of bondage — not to a corporate employer, but to an abstract ego construct. In my desperation to escape the caste system that brands me "ordinary," I had almost willingly locked the chains of a rival cage that demands I be extraordinary just to exist.

Returning to Hegel requires precision. Self-consciousness is permanently entangled with the recognition of others; no human identity is a solitary creation from the void. Yet that does not reduce an individual to a passive mirror of external judgment. The master-slave dialectic is not a comic-book skirmish of winners and losers — in Hegel's framework, the triumphant master fails to secure genuine recognition precisely because the subjugated bondsman lacks the free agency to confer it. Historical and social acknowledgment possesses immense weight, but it cannot be the load-bearing pillar of your entire reality. Global acclaim does not grant inward sovereignty, just as total obscurity cannot strip your capacity to build and act. The hunger for greatness can propel you to move mountains; mistaken for your essential core, it becomes a noose.

I have surrendered nothing. I still burn to forge work of historical weight; I still refuse to spend my life as an interchangeable cog; I still intend to break ground across serious research, engineering, and art; and I will still be shaken by minds possessing raw gifts I will never touch. The open challenge I keep before me is this: can one hunt greatness with ruthless aggression without making greatness the prerequisite for one's right to live? I don't have a neat formula, and I despise cheap platitudes — "everyone is special," "just love yourself." The line I hold is uncompromising: I will chase the extraordinary with everything I have, but the ultimate worth of my life will not be judged by whether I catch it.

That tension must remain raw and unsoftened — caught between the obsession to carve a name into the timeline and the freezing awareness of the odds. The vast majority of people will never be outliers. Realism can preserve your life, and realism can pave your tomb; idealism built civilizations, and ungrounded idealism manufactures psychosis. Often, simply to bring an impossible project into being, you have no choice but to run on a controlled placebo effect — because the brutal mechanics of reality will terrify you into paralysis before you take a step. Audacity beyond the regression line gives you the velocity to move, but it must never blind you to the cliff. Respect reality. Just don't worship it.

## A Wager on God

Studying historical databases shows that monumental achievement rarely hinges on intellect alone; it is bound to contingency — birth epoch, institutions, technological inflection points, and luck. Some with terrifying gifts are stranded in unfavorable eras; others catch a nascent wave and ride it into textbooks.

Tracing that boundary between individual will and structural contingency softened my skepticism: *perhaps there is something beyond the observable world that establishes its rules and witnesses what happens within them.*

I claim no scientific evidence for God, nor do I believe any specific religion holds an uncorrupted cosmic manual. My stance is philosophical: a transcendent reality cannot be ruled out, and deserves examination as an open metaphysical possibility.

By "God," I do not mean an anthropomorphic deity intervening on whim, performing miracles, or dispensing prizes and punishment. I consider an abstract ground: a higher intelligence establishing cosmic laws and witnessing reality without breaching physical regularities. Wars, poverty, technological revolutions, personal failures, and contingency unfold naturally — an open metaphysical possibility, not verified cosmology.

This invites Blaise Pascal’s inquiry ([SEP, *Pascal’s Wager*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pascal-wager/); [SEP, *Pragmatic Arguments and Belief in God*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatic-belief-god/)). Pascal evaluated belief under uncertainty through expected utility. Departing from infinite heavenly payoffs, I ask: *Even if God does not exist, can believing in God provide positive real-world utility to an individual?*

Consider the decision problem across two choices and two states of reality:

| Choice / Reality | God exists | God does not exist |
|---|---|---|
| I believe in God | Case I: Belief in a world with God | Case II: Belief in a world without God |
| I do not believe in God | Case III: Disbelief in a world with God | Case IV: Disbelief in a world without God |

In **Case I** (Belief when God exists), conviction aligns with metaphysical reality, framing cosmic order. Yet in a non-interventionist model, one cannot assume supernatural patronage or immortality; faith grants no exemption from physical laws.

**Case II** (Belief when God does not exist) is central: even in an empty universe, faith yields concrete secular utility. It resists despair over contingency, frames life against a grander horizon, and provides psychological order during crises, career setbacks, or cognitive limits. It inspires reflection on morality, responsibility, creation, and freedom, opens theological philosophy, and sustains hope when probabilities offer none. These benefits require no supernatural reality, though belief carries costs: cognitive bias, expended resources, or escapism. Net utility depends on balancing grounding against critical clarity.

In **Case III** (Disbelief when God exists), one holds an erroneous metaphysical view. Yet a non-interventionist creator would hardly demand tribal allegiance or punish honest skepticism, valuing authentic creation, moral integrity, and intellectual honesty above religious badges.

In **Case IV** (Disbelief when God does not exist), intellect aligns with a godless universe. Secular humanism, science, and art provide profound structures for meaning, ethics, and resilience. Disbelief is neither an ethical failure nor an existential handicap.

Case II is thus no automatic loss; faith can generate real philosophical and psychological value even without a divine listener.

Formalizing this decision baseline, let:
- \(p\): subjective probability assigned to God's existence.
- \(U_{BG}\): utility of belief when God exists.
- \(U_{BN}\): utility of belief when God does not exist.
- \(U_{NG}\): utility of disbelief when God exists.
- \(U_{NN}\): utility of disbelief when God does not exist.

The expected utilities are:

\[
EU(B)=pU_{BG}+(1-p)U_{BN}
\]

\[
EU(N)=pU_{NG}+(1-p)U_{NN}
\]

Under my personal values, where existential grounding outweighs friction without dogma, \(EU(B)>EU(N)\) can hold. Yet this is a pragmatic assessment, not a universal theorem: \(p > 0\) does not automatically mandate belief, nor assume divine rewards or punishments. Crucially: *the rationality of a belief as a practical choice is not the same as the truth of that belief.* Practical rationality evaluates utility; epistemic rationality demands truth. Practical utility proves nothing about ontology.

This reflection was sharpened by peers. In mathematics, physics, and engineering, I work alongside formidable technical minds who are practicing theists. Their existence dismantled the assumption that scientific sophistication precludes metaphysical conviction. Their faith does not prove God exists, nor represents the scientific community. It demonstrates that *scientific sophistication and metaphysical belief are not necessarily mutually exclusive.* One can demand empirical falsifiability in research while remaining open to unresolved metaphysical questions.

We cannot determine that God does not exist. Conceived as a transcendent ground of reality rather than an anomaly within it, empirical science lacks instruments for decisive disproof. Yet epistemic integrity requires an immediate boundary: *the absence of a proof of nonexistence is not evidence of existence.* Lack of disproof does not imply high probability. My stance remains a disciplined openness: acknowledging metaphysical possibilities while demanding strict scientific methodology in the physical world.

Even if God is a human construct, theology remains a vital resource for interrogating first principles ([SEP, *Philosophy of Religion*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/philosophy-religion/); [SEP, *Existentialism*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/existentialism/)):
- *Why is there something rather than nothing?*
- *Why does the universe have laws?*
- *What is consciousness?*
- *What makes a human life valuable?*
- *Why does suffering exist?*
- *Is morality discovered or constructed?*

These inquiries belong equally to metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, and philosophy of science. Theology does not provide scientific axioms, but it re-centers the question underlying technology, capital, and automation: *what is a human being ultimately for?*

This returns to existential anxiety. Confronting historical odds, cognitive ceilings, and technological displacement, faith offers no tactical rescue, intellectual shortcut, or economic shield. What it provides is willingness to confront an open question: even if I never secure the historical legacy I pursue, can a human life hold meaning beyond rankings, social validation, or religious labels? I leave that question open.

## Any system should be judged on one scale

Capital accumulation undeniably drove historic technological breakthroughs; founders have forged immense tangible value; markets remain unmatched at dynamic resource allocation; and the historical experiments pitched as alternatives brought catastrophic failures of their own. UBI, socialism, automated commonwealths — none is a frictionless panacea, and technology will never spontaneously generate equitable institutions. Productivity and distribution operate on different planes; mastering the former says nothing about the latter. Every rival -ism carries its own pathologies, and no system currently running on this planet represents a final answer. My metric is singularly focused: every institutional design must be weighed against the same criterion — does it convert technological productivity into actual human freedom? GDP tallies the volume of output, while staying perpetually silent on what that output was for, and who was actually liberated by it. By that gauge, the prevailing order earns a severely compromised verdict: it mints radical freedom for the few and bare subsistence for the rest. That ratio is the core political confrontation of this century, whether the establishment admits to asking it or not.

## A structural analysis of socialism and communism

My critique targets one failure alone: any institutional arrangement that fails to convert productive leaps into universal human freedom. Reading this as an endorsement of another camp is lazy thinking. It is worth analyzing the structural architecture of socialism and communism with precision.

Consider what socialism fundamentally aims for. Socialism represents a broad family of ideas concerning social ownership, redistribution, social safety nets, and democratic participation in production. Several of its core goals align with principles I have defended throughout this essay. Social protection: uncoupling a person's baseline human needs from their spot price on the labor market. Universal access to healthcare, education, and housing when systems are genuinely engineered to provide them. Collective risk-sharing: ensuring that an individual does not bear the solitary ruin of unemployment, illness, disability, an economic downturn, or a failed entrepreneurial attempt. The capacity to marshal resources toward projects with multidecadal horizons and zero short-term accounting returns — precisely the high-risk, foundational breakthroughs the market systematically starves. Curbing extreme private monopolies. And the core principle: that certain goods essential to human dignity should not be gated entirely by purchasing power. Finally, the promise that solid economic security provides the runway for individuals to study, create, conduct research, pivot careers, or build something of their own.

None of these goods is the exclusive copyright of socialism. Capitalist welfare regimes, public institutions, progressive taxation, universal social insurance, cooperatives, public ownership, and mixed market economies all target these outcomes. The meaningful question is not which ideological label wins the debate, but which concrete institutional designs reliably deliver these outcomes.

Examine socialism honestly, and its structural failure modes become glaring. These are not moral lapses of its champions; they are architectural breakdowns. The first is the information problem. Economic knowledge — shifting consumer desires, localized marginal costs, which soil can yield which industry — is dispersed across hundreds of millions of individuals and organizations; no central planning bureau can aggregate, compute, and act on this distributed data. The second is incentive decay: when personal reward decouples from effort and contribution, innovation, productivity, risk appetite, and accountability deteriorate, without anyone consciously sabotaging the system. The third is bureaucratic calcification: organizations established to coordinate resources inevitably spawn self-serving interests and drift from their founding mission. The fourth is the soft budget constraint: an enterprise that continues receiving state subsidies despite abysmal performance loses any pressure to adapt, streamline, or shut down. The fifth is the hyper-concentration of decision-making: when economic control fuses with political authority, economic miscalculations metastasize into political crises, and whoever controls the central plan controls far more than the plan. The sixth is the allocation puzzle: deciding what to produce, in what volume, and whose preferences to prioritize — a problem markets solve imperfectly yet automatically through price discovery. Underneath these problems lies the friction between collective goals and individual autonomy, alongside an uncomfortable historical reality: replacing private capital owners with a centralized administrative elite does not flatten hierarchy; it merely rotates the personnel at the top.

Acknowledging these flaws does not brand socialists as malicious or economically illiterate. It proves that central planning is an extraordinarily difficult engineering problem, and engineering difficulty is not a moral verdict.

A crucial boundary often gets lost in casual debate: socialism and communism are not interchangeable labels. Socialism, in my usage, refers to that broad family of structural reforms: collective ownership, redistribution, welfare guarantees, and worker participation. Communism in classical theory is a far more radical endpoint: universal common ownership, the total elimination of class distinctions, and, in Marxist doctrine, the withering away of the state as a coercive apparatus. Theoretical communism is distinct from the historical regimes that ruled under its name. I will neither evaluate an abstract ideal by its utopian brochure, nor dismiss every theoretical insight by pointing to historical totalitarian regimes. The gap between institutional blueprint and physical reality is a central theme of this essay — the same gap that divides the future we were promised from the future we mock.

This returns the argument to its starting point. In an era of scarcity, advising someone to "find a secure job first; survival precedes self-actualization" is entirely rational. But suppose AI, robotics, automated factories, fusion power, and advanced manufacturing drive the cost of producing essentials toward zero. The civilizational question shifts. If a society can generate abundant food, housing, transit, computation, and healthcare with minimal human labor, why should access to those goods remain contingent on an individual's ability to sell their labor on the market?

Here the historic debate between capitalism and socialism re-emerges with genuine urgency. It ceases to be an ideological battle over moral virtue and becomes an engineering challenge: what institutional framework successfully translates technological productivity into expanding human freedom?

Two boundaries must remain sharp throughout this transition. First, productivity is not distribution: a society's technical output can skyrocket while the returns remain concentrated behind corporate moats. Second, automation is not liberation: automation can mean shorter working hours, or it can mean soaring unemployment, extreme wealth divergence, disposable labor, or novel forms of digital servitude — or a mixture of all of them. Technology expands the set of possibilities; institutions dictate how much of that space reaches an ordinary person. I reject the naive techno-optimism that assumes AI will automatically solve inequality. It will do no such thing.

Survival rationality is protective under scarcity, but it has no business expanding into an all-encompassing philosophy of life. A society may pragmatically caution a young person that they need an income; yet it must also make space for the larger civilizational question: why should access to a meaningful, dignified life remain bound to how well one auctions off their labor? This question sits at the junction of capitalism, socialism, welfare models, UBI, automation, and post-scarcity civilization — and no existing system answers it well. Contemporary economic models excel at addressing the first two rationalities — how to stay solvent and how to maximize measurable outputs — while failing completely on the third: what is truly worth doing with a human life. An increasingly productive civilization must return the sovereignty of answering that third question to the individual. This is not a demand that everyone become an entrepreneur, an artist, or a scientist; on the contrary, a civilized society must never require an ordinary person to become extraordinary just to secure baseline autonomy. An ordinary human should be free to remain ordinary without their life being branded a failure.

Markets are formidable coordination engines, but they can generate extreme inequality, externalities, corporate monopolies, and setups where purchasing power decides who gets lifesaving medicine. Central planning can mobilize resources for collective goals, yet it runs straight into information black holes, incentive collapse, bureaucratic inertia, and power concentration. Welfare guarantees, public ownership, cooperatives, competitive markets, regulation, progressive taxation, and social insurance can be synthesized in countless configurations. Institutions are technologies, carrying trade-offs like any other design. The real task is determining which institutional architecture produces the lowest friction and the greatest human freedom at a given level of productive capacity, computational throughput, and social complexity.

The metric remains the scale proposed at the start. A civilization must not measure progress solely by GDP totals, corporate quarterly reports, market capitalization, employment figures, or output per worker. It must confront one question: how much freedom does each additional unit of technological productivity deliver to an ordinary human being? If a hundred humanoid robots replace a hundred factory workers, the vital question is not how much the profit margin expanded. It is what happened to the time, autonomy, security, and possibilities of those hundred displaced people. The ultimate justification for driving productivity cannot be maximizing output for its own sake; it is expanding the number of people who no longer have to sell their lives to survive — and leaving them free to decide what living is for.

## A Generation Caught in a Non-War Catastrophe

I want to speak directly to the generation absorbing this disruption in their bones.

We may be living through a historical catastrophe that involves no war: no artillery salvos, no bombed-out city blocks, no invading armies. Data center cooling towers hum through the night, AI benchmarks break records every quarter, and tech conglomerates mint trillions. Yet behind this digital spectacle, the careers, families, and life trajectories that an entire generation should have built are being severed at the root. Catastrophes don't require smoking ruins: prolonged unemployment, cratering entry-level wages, devalued degrees, evaporating promotion ladders, and skills honed over years made obsolete overnight by a single update — these are slow-motion catastrophes.

If you hold a degree from a top university, carry a solid resume, and possess real technical or academic ability, yet the only jobs open to you in major cities across mainland China or the United States pay two to five dollars an hour, do not assume you are incompetent. You were thrown into an unforgiving historical trough. A person's capability and their market compensation are fundamentally different variables: macroeconomic cycles, industrial realignment, automation, labor supply shocks, and geography all dictate compensation. Not every hardship can be blamed on the era; personal choices matter. But a depressed wage is not a final verdict on your intellect, grit, or potential. Earning two to five dollars an hour is an aberration produced by an economic deep freeze, not a legitimate baseline for a healthy society.

I worry that what is happening now is more structurally treacherous than Japan after its 1991 bubble burst. That collapse triggered decades of stagnation that weighed down a generation. Today’s youth face a deadlier combination of structural headwinds: slowing global growth, brutal industrial competition, cognitive labor replacement driven by generative AI, extreme credential inflation, and an accelerating divide between capital returns and labor compensation. In certain respects, the long-term scar tissue on this generation may run deeper than Japan's lost decades. This is an analogy, not a settled verdict. The colder conviction I hold is this: an entire generation can see its future slip away without hearing a single shot fired.

The cruelest dimension of this crisis is the irreversible arrow of time. An economic cycle eventually bottoms out; technological shocks get absorbed over decades; new industries establish footing and resume hiring. But a human life does not bounce back in a V-shaped curve on an earnings chart. If this deep freeze lasts five years, a graduate loses their launching pad. If it drags on for twenty, it consumes their prime creative years. And if the shock waves linger across a lifetime, then even if society achieves extraordinary prosperity half a century later, it will not compensate for the stolen youth of those who lived through it. They will carry that loss to the grave, never knowing what they might have built in an expanding era. I offer no platitudes that everyone will recover, because the reality is that many will not.

If historical circumstance robs you of the future you should have had, you have every right to confront fate. If an economic tsunami you never controlled pulls your life into the mud, you have the right to be angry — at fate, at the era, at God, in an existential sense rather than a theological one. You do not have to swallow your pain to appear resilient or mature. But standing amid the rubble, how you navigate what remains — whether you keep creating, whether you salvage meaning — remains yours to decide. No moralizer has the standing to demand that you forgive this era, and I will not insult your struggle with the cliché that suffering makes you stronger.

Step back and look at the world we are constructing: does it not resemble an unvarnished dystopia? Under the same sky, a small cadre of AI researchers, founders, and early equity holders hold fortunes worth tens or hundreds of millions of dollars, while ordinary workers survive on five hundred dollars a month — an index of structural poverty, not a median wage statistic. One pole of the system commands compute grids, capital reserves, automated pipelines, and massive windfalls; the other fights an exhausting battle for next month’s rent. The question that refuses to go away is structural: why does a civilization capable of such technological feats permit so many people to lose their economic security and personal autonomy?

Civilizational milestones and individual tragedies are not mutually exclusive. A technological revolution can write triumphant chapters in macroeconomic history while crushing the careers of millions over several decades. A rising GDP graph does not mean wages find their way into your pocket; soaring productivity metrics do not reduce your working hours; collective paper wealth does not support the people who absorb the costs of transition. A civilization can surge ahead on technological benchmarks while narrowing the horizons of an entire generation; the wealth accumulated at the end of the line will never undo the quiet destruction of the lives it ran over.

I cannot promise this generation that compensation will come, nor can I guarantee that I will not end up among those sidelined by the shock. But I reject the premise that losing your footing in an economic storm diminishes your personal worth. A talented, dedicated young person who runs into a wall because they were born into the wrong historical window has not failed as an individual. That principle is the core of this essay.

## The Fear of Supporting Humanity

In the average worker's financial horizon, one hundred million yuan — roughly fourteen million dollars — is an astronomical sum; an ordinary employee working continuously could rarely expect to touch it in a lifetime. Yet inspecting the balance sheets of global tech firms, quantitative market makers, energy traders, and industrial conglomerates reveals a different scale: the real economic architecture is orders of magnitude larger than the sliver visible to the public. A fortune that looks like the finish line of financial independence inside a single life is a rounding error inside global capital. That difference in scale changed how I view wealth, scarcity, and the value of human labor.

Take Anthropic. By 2026, it employs thousands of people, and its surging valuation and equity packages have minted paper wealth unimaginable to an average worker. In November 2026, Reuters reported via The Star that its anticipated IPO is set to produce thousands of paper millionaires among current and former staff ([Reuters, via The Star](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2026/11/19/anthropic-ipo-to-mint-thousands-of-millionaires)). Unmonetized equity is not liquid cash. The eye-opening fact is the structural divergence: income earned from selling physical or cognitive hours and wealth generated from holding equity in an exponential asset exist in different universes. Owning a tiny fraction of the right company in the right wave easily eclipses decades of professional labor by credentialed specialists — an outcome driven by ownership structures, entry timing, network position, and capital market leverage, rather than raw grit or intellectual superiority.

A childhood friend of one of my close friends works at Anthropic. That detail opened a crack in my reality. Anthropic stopped being an abstract ticker in financial reporting; it became a concrete reality just two degrees of separation from my immediate life. I know nothing of his title, equity, compensation, or daily tasks. What struck me was the contrast between social proximity and economic distance. Someone connected to my personal network had entered the engine room of the AI revolution, while many peers of our generation struggle to land any stable, reasonably compensated job. We live in the same era, move through connected social networks, yet access entirely different universes of capital, opportunity, and technology. The topological distance between two nodes in a social network can be negligible, while the economic chasm between their lives is vast. Employment there guarantees no financial freedom. The realization is simpler: the vast disparities of modern capital broke into my life through a real human relationship.

Now look at the AI world we actually have: people in the Bay Area's AI companies pulling in over a hundred million a year, while even ordinary graduates of a C9 university take home a few thousand yuan a month. It feels like the Huangpu River in the 1930s. Along the Bund's exquisite buildings walk foreign merchants in gorgeous clothes — prominent, certainly, and yet still not historical figures. And right beside those merchants, out on the water, are the Chinese boat trackers, starving.

The corporations operating at this scale are often invisible to consumers. Most people recognize consumer-facing brands like Apple, Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Meta, or Tesla. But beneath the surface lie massive commercial giants that the general public has never encountered — a gap that can be particularly wide between public perception in mainland China and global capital flows. This can be understood as the gap between economic scale and public visibility — an observation based on economic reality, not a formal survey.

Consider global commodity and energy trading. Vitol, the world’s largest independent energy trader, posted turnover of roughly 343 billion dollars in 2025, with net profits reportedly around 4.5 billion ([Vitol](https://www.vitol.com/vitol-2025-volumes-and-review/)). Turnover differs from net profit: commodity traders move immense physical volumes on narrow margins. Trafigura reported revenues of roughly 240 billion dollars for fiscal 2025 ([Trafigura](https://www.trafigura.com/news-and-insights/insights/2025-trafigura-annual-results/financial-review/)). Add Cargill, a private titan of global agriculture. A consumer who uses Netflix and Spotify every day may never hear of Vitol, yet the physical commodities Vitol trades move hundreds of billions of dollars across global supply chains each year.

Look at quantitative trading and automated market making. Citadel operates as a premier multi-strategy hedge fund manager; Citadel Securities, run independently, stands as one of the largest market makers in global finance. Jane Street is an algorithmic pillar of market liquidity. These firms do not buy consumer billboards: their presence rests on proprietary capital, low-latency algorithms, dedicated trading infrastructure, and access to deep financial liquidity. They maintain lean teams, so their capital density per employee bears no resemblance to traditional, labor-intensive enterprises. Assets under management (AUM) do not equal corporate equity, let alone a manager’s net worth, but the capital leverage involved is extraordinary.

Examine the layer of enterprise software and digital infrastructure. ServiceNow underpins the operational workflows of multinational corporations; Constellation Software expands by acquiring mission-critical, vertical-market software providers; Equinix operates the data centers and physical cross-connects through which global internet traffic flows. Consumers rely on services running over this architecture every day without learning the names behind it. The global economy is built on infrastructural backbones that operate far away from consumer brand awareness.

Consider industrial manufacturing, semiconductor EDA, and supply chain logistics. Synopsys builds the software used to design microprocessors with billions of transistors; Amphenol manufactures connectors and interconnect assemblies used everywhere from automobiles to aerospace; McKesson manages pharmaceutical and medical supply distribution. These companies rarely make products that provoke public debate, yet they form the physical infrastructure supporting modern healthcare, transport, and computation. In engineering circles, these names are foundational; the gap exists between industry dominance and general public recognition.

Look at the frontier wave in artificial intelligence. Mercor, which matches specialized domain experts with AI developers for model training and alignment, reached an estimated valuation of 20 billion dollars in November 2026 ([Staffing Industry Analysts](https://www.staffingindustry.com/news/global-daily-news/mercor-lands-750m-funding-round-valuing-staffing-platform-at-20b)); Databricks operates enterprise data and AI infrastructure; Anduril redesigns defense hardware using autonomous systems. Some remain private, while others are publicly traded. They are established within their sectors, yet remain largely invisible to the public. Our perception of productive capacity is shaped by consumer visibility rather than the actual structure of the global industrial economy.

When this level of private wealth is placed alongside a municipal economy, the comparison is striking. By 2026, Elon Musk’s personal net worth was estimated near one trillion dollars, with post-SpaceX IPO estimates surpassing 1.1 trillion. Shanghai’s GDP in 2025 reached approximately 5.67 trillion yuan, roughly 814 billion dollars ([Xinhua](http://english.scio.gov.cn/pressroom/2026-01/21/content_118292482.html)). One individual's paper net worth now rivals or exceeds the annual economic output of an international metropolis of over twenty million people — though comparing a capital asset stock to an annual flow of value added is not a direct balance-sheet comparison. The point is simpler: the absolute scale of modern capital accumulation has outpaced our everyday intuitions about money.

This gives Liu Cixin’s short story *Supporting Humanity* (赡养人类) renewed relevance. The story imagines a civilization where property rights concentrate under market forces until a single "ultimate owner" holds title to the planet's productive assets — a thought experiment exploring extreme property institutions rather than literal prophecy. Yet its central question grows sharper: when productive capacity can comfortably guarantee the baseline needs of an entire species, why should the majority lack reliable access to basic resources? As AI, robotics, energy production, and automated manufacturing expand, that question cannot be dismissed as science fiction.

Whenever public debate turns to universal basic income (UBI), comprehensive social safety nets, or automated resource dividends, predictable objections arise. If survival no longer requires wage labor, who will work? Who covers the tax burden? Who decides how resources are allocated? Without the threat of poverty, will civilization stop innovating? These concerns raise genuine economic and institutional design challenges that must be addressed carefully. But underneath these practical questions sits a deep psychological inertia built over centuries of scarcity. Humanity has learned to celebrate machines replacing human labor, yet struggles to imagine a life where survival is not tied to compulsory work. In an economy defined by physical scarcity, tying basic security to labor made sense; as technology lowers the labor cost of producing essentials, our institutional software should evolve alongside it. I describe this resistance as the fear of supporting humanity. That unease is not confined to capital owners; many ordinary workers feel that unconditional security is unfair or encourages dependency. We have tied personal dignity to labor income for so long that when machines become capable of handling that work, we struggle to separate the right to live from participation in wage labor.

A distinction must be made here to keep this analysis grounded: an individual whose net worth is valued at one trillion dollars does not hold that sum in liquid currency. Paper valuations cannot be directly converted into antibiotics, housing, or clean energy, and a one-time redistribution of billionaire assets would not sustain civilization over the long run. Enduring stability rests on physical productive capacity — dependable power grids, industrialized construction, healthcare systems, automated logistics, and manufacturing — guided by institutions that coordinate those assets effectively. Capital represents a claim on future productivity, not the physical goods themselves. The central civilizational question is not "why don't we confiscate private wealth," but rather: why has translating technological abundance into universal human autonomy not become a primary engineering priority for our civilization?

Philanthropic initiatives, welfare models, and local basic-income trials exist, and their efforts matter. Yet our civilization directs vast resources into frontier AI compute clusters, interplanetary rocketry, extreme ultraviolet lithography, and fusion research, while the goal of reducing humanity's dependence on wage labor for survival is rarely treated with equal seriousness. We design complex compute networks, build semiconductor manufacturing facilities costing hundreds of billions of dollars, and explore deep-space propulsion — while our public imagination regarding economic autonomy remains trapped in familiar arguments over budget deficits and moral hazard. Rocketry budgets cannot be moved into welfare lines overnight. It is a question about civilizational priorities and institutional ambition, not a simple bookkeeping adjustment.

This leads back to the fundamental question: what is technological progress ultimately for? If artificial intelligence accelerates productivity while displacing workers without opening new paths to security, who does the revolution liberate? If automated systems generate immense wealth while ordinary people must spend their lives working simply to cover rent and medical bills, technological progress remains incomplete. This is not an argument against wealth creation, nor against rewarding entrepreneurs, scientists, and engineers for real contributions. It is a rejection of treating labor-market outcomes as the final measure of human dignity and freedom. A great civilization should be capable of achieving several goals simultaneously: securing baseline survival and freedom for everyone, maintaining incentives for innovation, protecting individual autonomy, and freeing more people to create, explore, and build lives of their own choosing.

Perhaps the deepest unease within an advanced civilization is not that machines will become capable of supporting humanity, but that humanity will have to confront an uncomfortable historical truth: that for generations, we accepted an arrangement where the right to exist was made conditional on selling our labor.

## The Gradient-Driven Expectation Field

I have tried, more than once, to describe the gap between what technology actually is and what people expect it to become. At some point I stopped writing sentences and tried to write an equation. The result is a crude model I call the gradient-driven expectation field. It is my own exploratory construction, not an empirically validated law, and I will say plainly at the end what it cannot do.

The first assumption is that the actual capability of a technology within one paradigm follows a logistic curve:

$$S(t)=\frac{L}{1+e^{-k(t-t_0)}}$$

\(S(t)\) is the capability at time \(t\), \(L\) the ceiling of this particular paradigm, \(k\) the growth parameter, \(t_0\) the inflection point where growth peaks. \(L\) is not the final limit of human technology; it is the limit of one paradigm, one performance metric, one S-curve.

The growth rate is \(v(t)=S'(t)\), and the relative growth rate is \(\lambda(t)=S'(t)/S(t)\). The relative rate is the thing humans actually feel. The model's core claim is that people extrapolate from it:

$$P(t,\tau)=S(t)e^{\lambda(t)\tau}$$

At time \(t\), people predict the capability at \(t+\tau\) by extending the current relative growth rate exponentially forward. This is bounded rationality: the local slope is treated as if it were a law of the whole future.

But how far forward people dare to extend the trend is not fixed. When growth is explosive, the social imagination becomes more aggressive, so the extrapolation horizon becomes a function of the growth rate:

$$R(t)=R_{\max}\left(\frac{S'(t)}{\max S'(t)}\right)^{\beta}$$

\(R(t)\) is the effective horizon, \(R_{\max}\) the maximum allowed extrapolation, and \(\beta>0\) the sensitivity of the horizon to the growth rate. This \(R(t)\) is an assumption inside the model, not a prediction horizon measured in psychology experiments.

Combining the horizon with the current relative growth rate gives the expectation field:

$$E(t)=S(t)e^{\lambda(t)R(t)}=S(t)\exp\left[R_{\max}\frac{S'(t)^{1+\beta}}{S(t)(\max S'(t))^\beta}\right]$$

\(E(t)\) is the future capability the public expects, expressed on the same capability scale as \(S(t)\) — not a stock price, not a market value, not public satisfaction.

For a single logistic stage, with \(s(t)=S(t)/L\), this reduces to the core form of the model:

$$E(t)=Ls(t)\exp\left[kR_{\max}(1-s(t))\left(4s(t)(1-s(t))\right)^{\beta}\right]$$

The gap between expectation and reality is:

$$B(t)=E(t)-S(t+R(t))$$

When \(B(t)>0\), the public expected more than what arrives; when \(B(t)<0\), it underestimated the technology. \(B(t)\) is an abstract proxy for an expectation bubble — not a real financial bubble, which would also involve capital costs, liquidity, competition, investment incentives and asset pricing.

The most important behavior the model permits is this: \(S'(t)>0\) does not imply \(E'(t)>0\). The technology can continue improving while society becomes increasingly disappointed in it. After a fast-growth phase, the relative growth rate falls, the horizon \(R(t)\) collapses, and the expectations formed during the boom stop being met. The technology is objectively stronger than five years ago, and the public conversation is more pessimistic than five years ago. Nothing in the technology stopped; only the second derivative of the expectation did. I am saying the model allows this dynamic, not that it predicts it for every technology.

A single logistic cannot describe technologies that cross paradigms, so the capability can be written as a sum of stages:

$$S(t)=\sum_i \frac{L_i}{1+e^{-k_i(t-t_i)}}$$

with \(v(t)=S'(t)\), \(R(t)=R_{\max}\left(v(t)/\sup v(t)\right)^{\beta}\), and

$$E(t)=S(t)\exp\left[R_{\max}\frac{v(t)^{1+\beta}}{S(t)(\sup v(t))^\beta}\right]$$

where \(\sup v(t)\) is the supremum of the growth rate over the studied interval. This allows several rounds of breakthrough, expectation inflation and disappointment: theory first, then commercialization, then a new architecture that starts the next cycle. That different stages add up is a modeling assumption, not a claim that every technological revolution obeys logistic curves.

Against the AI present, the model reads naturally. The early capability jumps of large language models, the steady gains attributed to scaling laws, and the arrival of agents, world models and embodied AI each move the perceived growth rate, and with it the horizon \(R(t)\). When a stage improves quickly, society over-extrapolates the short trend into radical expectations about AGI, automation and job displacement; when a route hits constraints of cost, data, compute, reliability or real-world interaction, the expectation falls — while the technology itself keeps improving. A new paradigm can then re-ignite the cycle. The model does not say AI will hit a wall, and it does not say AGI arrives in any particular year. It describes how expectations are formed. It is not an oracle.

Which connects the model to the rest of this essay. Society's imagination of AI, robotics, UBI and post-scarcity civilization is not strictly synchronized with the actual pace of technology. In fast phases the public believes a fabulously rich future is imminent; when growth slows, or the gains are distributed unevenly, or institutions lag behind the technology, the same public swings toward disappointment. Three variables must be separated: technological capability, public expectation, and institutional distribution. My model describes the relationship between the first two. The third is not decided by any of these formulas; it needs its own economic and institutional theory ([SEP, *Philosophy of Technology*](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/technology/)). And that is the essay's central claim in mathematical dress: a civilization may overestimate how quickly technology will transform society, while simultaneously underestimating how profoundly it may eventually change the conditions of human life. The short term gets overpriced and the long term gets underpriced, and between the two sits an institutional problem no gradient can solve.

The limitations, stated plainly. This is a conceptual model with no empirical validation yet. A technology's capability \(S(t)\) is hard to define as a single comparable scalar. Public expectations are driven by media, capital markets, politics, culture and personal experience, not only by the growth rate. The dependence of \(R(t)\) on the growth rate is a hypothesis waiting for data. Real growth can be exponential, power-law, stepwise, logistic, or some combination. The parameters would have to be estimated from real data, not set by intuition, and the existence of an equation does not prove any psychological or sociological law. The model is related to the familiar technology hype cycle, but it tries to give that cycle an explicit dynamical mechanism rather than a slogan. It is not an academically established theory.

The framework makes exactly one testable claim: if s keeps rising for a decade while E never crashes and no expectation gap appears anywhere, the model is wrong.

## Why the old narratives hold on

Why do old narratives persist long after the material base has shifted? Because they are not purely economic. They are rooted in human nature. Consider "worldly wisdom" (人情世故): it is hard-coded into our evolutionary makeup. Over evolutionary history, humans developed emotional mechanisms to survive: constrained by Dunbar’s number, our cognitive bandwidth limits close relational ties to a small band. Societies rely on shared trust, status hierarchies, and ritual rather than pure reason. 人情世故 functions as an operating system built on human biology. A society does not discard an established operating system the moment a better alternative appears on paper; it patches it. Survival-first pragmatism, ROI calculations, and cynical risk management persist because they have functioned as structural supports. They help manage the fear of poverty, navigate social standing, handle family commitments, and soften the uncertainty of the future. Civilization updates its tools first and its core narratives last. This lag is not an unexpected flaw; it is biological firmware shaped by generations of survival pressure. Humanity commands the power to reshape planetary geography and deploy global computation, yet our collective instincts remain near the fire of an ancestral camp, guarding against scarcity.

### No one changes the world alone

A vital correction against the isolation of this struggle: no individual transforms the world entirely alone. The architecture of civilization is built by many people working in parallel through history. Prominent entrepreneurs may speak of having transformed the world single-handedly, yet Musk’s core proposals were explored by researchers decades before him — and even Einstein’s work rested on the foundations laid by Maxwell, Lorentz, Poincaré, and many others. Refusing to settle for a statistical average does not turn someone into a solitary hero. Every breakthrough is shaped by networks of peers, institutional scaffolding, historical timing, and chance events; personal will provides the spark, but it remains one variable in a wider equation.

### The gravity of reality

As Liu Cixin observed, every soaring thought must eventually crash down under the gravity of reality. That observation is not an admission of defeat. It highlights an engineering problem: between ideals and physical reality lies an interface that requires calibration, testing, and continuous work — holding fast to the ideal while respecting reality's constraints. Most attempts will fail, but the high casualty rate is precisely why the few who persist matter. That is why "don't get above yourself" is such a damaging piece of advice: it optimizes individual survival, but pushed to its logical conclusion, it guarantees civilizational stagnation. Humanity may sit at the edge of the Great Filter, yet through every crisis, the bugs have never been wiped out — and somewhere around an unseen corner, a black swan may emerge to help humanity navigate a path forward through the mud.

## Conclusion

I know reality is savage. Most attempts will crash; resource distribution is warped to the bone; talent and raw luck tilt the table mercilessly; "survive first" is frequently the smartest move on the board; Homejoy proved beyond dispute that unchecked ideals get obliterated by operations; cynicism seduces because its grim forecasts are statistically vindicated every single day; and even placebo self-belief won't alter the math of the house.

Yet reality is no oracle. Probability is not destiny. A tactical survival guide is not the purpose of existence, and full employment is no trophy for a civilization. Explosive productive power should never mean merely running on the treadmill with greater efficiency; it must mean steadily shrinking the head count of people coerced into auctioning their lives for bare breath. A civilization earns its greatness on a single question: does it deliver to ordinary humans the real power to choose what they live for?

Respect reality, but don't worship it. Reality should draw the tactical parameters of the plan, not the horizon of the imagination. Aggregate statistics trace the broad herd; they hold zero title over the fate of an individual. And no one should ever have to become extraordinary just to earn the right to steer their own life.
